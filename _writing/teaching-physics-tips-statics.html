Basic theory

Equilibrium of a Particle

  • A particle is in equilibrium if the net force acting on it is zero:

    \[\sum \vec{F} = 0\]

  • This means no acceleration; the particle is at rest or moving at constant velocity.

Equilibrium of a Rigid Body

  • For a rigid body in equilibrium:

    • The net force is zero:

      \[\sum \vec{F} = 0\]

    • The net moment (torque) about any point is zero:

      \[\sum \vec{M} = 0\]

Moments (Torque)

  • Moment about a point O is the turning effect of a force:

    \[M = F d\]

Where:

  • F = magnitude of force (N)

  • d = perpendicular distance from point O to the line of action of F

  • Direction: clockwise or anticlockwise moments

Couple

  • A couple consists of two equal and opposite forces whose lines of action do not coincide.

  • Moment of a couple:

    \[M = F \times d\]

  • Where d is the perpendicular distance between the forces.

Types of Forces in Statics

  • Tension: Force that pulls/stretch (e.g. in a rope).

  • Compression: Force that pushes/squeezes.

  • Normal reaction: Perpendicular force exerted by a surface.

  • Friction: Force resisting motion, parallel to the surface.

Resultant Force

  • The single force which has the same effect as a system of forces.

  • Calculated by vector addition of all forces:

    \[\vec{R} = \sum \vec{F_i}\]

Resolution of Forces

  • Forces can be resolved into components, often horizontal (x) and vertical (y):

    \[F_x = F \cos \theta \quad , \quad F_y = F \sin \theta\]

Centre of Mass

  • The point at which the entire weight of a body can be considered to act.

  • Found by taking moments of weights or using geometric symmetry.

Stories to enritch

The leaning tower

The Leaning Tower That Should Have Fallen (But Didn’t)

Few structures look more obviously unstable than the Leaning Tower of Pisa. From the moment students see a photograph, the instinctive reaction is the same: How has that not fallen over? The tower leans by several degrees, its centre visibly displaced from the vertical, and yet it has stood for over eight centuries. To many learners, this feels like a violation of common sense.

The story begins with a mistake. Built on soft, compressible ground, the tower started leaning almost as soon as construction began. As additional floors were added, the lean increased, and builders attempted to correct it by subtly altering the heights of later storeys. The result is a tower that is not only leaning, but slightly curved — an architectural record of centuries of attempts to keep it standing.

The physics, however, is unforgivingly simple. The tower does not fall because its centre of mass still lies above its base of support. As long as the vertical line through the centre of mass falls within the footprint of the base, there is no unbalanced turning moment to cause toppling. The tower may look precarious, but it is in rotational equilibrium.

This makes the tower an ideal context for teaching moments. Students often believe that leaning automatically means falling. In reality, what matters is not the angle, but whether gravity produces a turning effect about the base. The Leaning Tower survives not because it is strong, but because the torque due to its weight does not yet overcome the resisting moment provided by the ground.

The story becomes even richer when modern intervention is introduced. In the late 20th century, engineers deliberately removed small amounts of soil from beneath the higher side of the tower. Counter-intuitively, this made the tower lean less. By carefully adjusting the position of the centre of mass without adding visible supports, they reduced the turning moment and increased stability — a real-world demonstration that equilibrium can be altered without changing the object itself.

The Wobbly Café Table and the Mathematician’s Trick

In a café near CERN, a physicist grew tired of wobbling tables. Rather than constantly asking for a new table, he demonstrated that by rotating a four-legged table on uneven floor, you can always find a position where all four legs touch the ground simultaneously. This isn’t magic — it’s a consequence of mathematical continuity and equilibrium: a balance-seeking property of the tabletop’s legs on irregular surfaces. The idea can be explored visually and makes a great discussion starter on what it means for a system to have an equilibrium configuration.

⚙️ 2. The Gyroscope in a Suitcase (The “Devil’s Suitcase”)

French physicist Jean Perrin once reportedly packed a running aviation gyroscope into a suitcase and checked it in at a Paris station. When a porter tried to turn a corner, the suitcase resisted turning and seemed to exert a twisting moment on him. The porter reportedly exclaimed that “the devil himself must be inside!” This playful anecdote beautifully illustrates torque and rotational inertia — how spinning objects resist changes to their orientation.

Teaching connection: You can explore why spinning objects resist torque, relate to bicycles, skateboards, and even satellites maintaining orientation.

🎢 3. Rollercoaster Forces and Acceleration

Rollercoasters aren’t just thrilling — they are full of physics. At the top of a hill, riders feel pushed forward by tangential forces, and at the bottom, they feel pushed backward. Different cars on the same train experience these forces slightly differently depending on their position. This offers a real-world way to talk about forces, acceleration, inertia, and how net forces and motion relate.

🧠 4. The Inverted Pendulum and Balancing Acts

The inverted pendulum — like balancing a broom on your fingertip — is a classic physics challenge. Unlike a normal pendulum, which is stable when hanging down, an inverted one (center of mass above pivot) is unstable without active balancing. Engineers use this principle in Segways and self-balancing robots, where sensors and control systems constantly correct torque to keep the system upright.

Teaching connection: Ask students why upright balancing is harder than hanging — this connects center of mass, torque, and stability.

🪩 5. Shoelace Acceleration — 7 g in Everyday Life

Researchers studying why shoelaces come undone found that end-of-shoe impacts and the motion of the lace ends can briefly expose the knot to accelerations around seven times gravity — similar to what spacecraft experience on re-entry. This unexpected fact grabs attention and connects everyday experience with forces and acceleration.

🤸‍♂️ 6. Dance, Balance, and Torque (Dua Lipa / Basketball Lean)

Physics appears in performance arts too. In a well-known video analysis, a dancer on a rotating platform leans far back without falling. It seems to defy balance, but the explanation lies in effective forces in a rotating frame — where centripetal effects and friction generate balancing torque. On a different note, analyses of basketball player Kyrie Irving’s lean show how center of mass and torque limit how far a person can lean before falling — unless aided by external constraints.

Teaching connection: Use videos to show torque and center-of-mass in action and discuss why athletes can or cannot maintain certain postures.

Practical work and Demos

Balancing forks and a pivot mechanism

The position of the centre of gravity of a system is vital for its stability. Fix two forks together, prongs to prongs, stick a match stick through between the prongs and then balance the arrangement on the edge of a glass or mug. For an extra twist, you can set fire to the end of the matchstick over the mug. The wood burns, eventually going out at the edge of the mug, leaving the forks balanced.

Balancing a pencil and/or a snooker cue

Why is it so much more difficult to balance a pencil than a snooker cue on your finger? This can be demonstrated by using a number of differently shaped rods - long, short, light, and heavy with a mass at the top or near the lower end - a pencil and a snooker cue. It is much easier to balance heavy rods or ones with a mass at the top such as a mop!

The ease of balancing is simply to do with how sensitively you can adjust the position of the centre of mass - a small movement of your hand will produce a large angular acceleration in a light rod.

The Box & the Balloon

At first glance, the setup looks almost mischievous. A cardboard box overhangs the edge of a table, apparently held in place by nothing more than a helium balloon tied to one side. The visual cue is irresistible: students immediately assume the balloon is doing the work. It looks as though the balloon must be supporting the box, counteracting its weight and preventing it from tipping. That assumption is precisely what makes the demonstration so effective.

When students are asked what is supporting the box, the answer comes quickly and confidently. The balloon is visible, unusual, and visually aligned with the overhanging side. The real mechanism is hidden, and therefore ignored. At this point, no correction is offered. The misconception is allowed to settle.

The first moment of challenge comes when the string is cut. The balloon floats harmlessly away, yet the box remains exactly where it is. Nothing collapses, nothing shifts, and the expected drama fails to materialise. This moment of stillness is powerful. It forces students to confront the fact that their explanation cannot be correct. If the balloon were supporting the box, its removal would have caused the system to fail. It does not.

Only now is attention redirected to what is actually happening. The table edge acts as a pivot. Inside the box, hidden from view, are heavy masses placed deliberately in the corner resting on the table. The weight of these masses produces a moment about the pivot that balances the moment produced by the overhanging section of the box. The system is in rotational equilibrium, not because of an upward force, but because clockwise and anticlockwise moments are equal.

When the box is opened and the masses revealed, the physics becomes tangible. Students can see that the apparent simplicity of the setup was deceptive. The balloon, which seemed central to the explanation, played no mechanical role at all. It was a red herring — a visual distraction that encouraged an intuitive but incorrect attribution of cause.

The demonstration can then be deepened with a second reveal. If the box is taped firmly to the desk and the masses are removed, the system once again remains stable even when the balloon is cut away. This reinforces a further idea: support does not always come from visible objects acting upwards. Constraints, fixings, and reaction moments can all prevent motion without resembling the kinds of “support” students expect.

What this demonstration exposes is not a lack of knowledge, but a habit of reasoning. Students tend to explain equilibrium by pointing to the most conspicuous object in the scene. They confuse proximity with causation and contact with support. By carefully removing the balloon and later revealing the hidden masses, the demonstration forces a more disciplined approach: identify the pivot, identify the forces, consider their distances, and only then decide what is actually responsible for equilibrium.

In this way, the balloon is invaluable precisely because it does nothing. It creates a compelling false explanation that must be dismantled using moments, free-body reasoning, and careful thought. The result is a much deeper understanding of rotational equilibrium — and a healthy suspicion of explanations that rely on what merely looks right.

Weighing Air

It must be appreciated that air is a ‘real substance’. But air, being invisible, is known by the

indirect observation of its effects. Therefore we must be able to appreciate that air has

mass; meaning that it occupies space, and is attracted by gravity—an effect we generally

describe as weight. We should be able to show juniors that air takes up space and is capable

of being weighed, but it is doubtful that young children will be able to grasp the

significance of mass. These ideas can be explored with a simple piece of apparatus.

Impale a small clipboard clip A on a 5 cm nail. Fix the nail, pointing sideways, into a

cork. Insert the cork in a heavy bottle. Grip a 1 m long cm? section balsawood strip in the

clip, near one end of the wood. Dimensions given are not critical. Then tape a bent wire

‘hook’ B at the longer end of the strip, and attach some clothes pegs, or modelling clay C, on

the shorter end, so that the strip roughly balances. Use a cardboard balloon pump to inflate

a very large round balloon, and seal its neck with a ‘twist’ of light wire.

Stand the bottle-and-balsa ‘balance’ on a stool in an area where there are no draughts.

Suspend the big balloon by hitching the wire upon the hook. Adjust the balance of the

apparatus, until the balsa ‘beam’ is horizontal. Children might like to suggest alternative

ways to get it to balance. Inevitably the balloon will have acquired a charge of static

electricity. This nuisance can be turned to advantage, through discussion of the difficulty

with the children. Next, the balloon must be removed, without disturbing the beam.

While the balloon is being deflated, a child can support the beam, using a finger.

Afterwards, the balloon envelope is hung back on the hook by the same wire. Then, when

the child’s finger is removed, the beam will be seen to be slanting, with its longer balloon

carrying end somewhat raised. Clearly the balloon, having lost the air it enclosed, is now

less heavy than before. It has lost weight, proving that it must have ‘had weight’ when the

experiment began. Of course the test is repeated several times.

The value of the apparatus lies mostly in the questions it provokes. Why did we use a

balsawood beam that was much longer on one side? Perhaps our young scientists will object

that the experiment proves nothing, because the weight of the air inside the balloon is

equalled by the buoyant upthrust of the air around it. (It might be hoped that very young

children would not notice this anomaly . . .) Of course, the mass of air we have investi-

gated was the difference between compressed air inside the balloon and the less dense air

outside.

Statics off the page

Setting up a “circus” of real-life statics problems is particularly powerful because it turns abstract exam questions into tangible experiences. Instead of just resolving forces on paper, students move between stations where they can see and feel equilibrium in action. For example, one station might involve a cantilevered ruler clamped to a desk with masses added at different distances, forcing students to predict when it will tip and to calculate moments about the pivot. Another might use stacked wooden blocks or domino structures, where students must decide how load is distributed and why a structure remains stable even when parts are removed. A third station could involve a beam supported by two spring balances, allowing students to measure reaction forces directly and compare them with calculated values. Even simple setups—like hanging masses from strings at angles or balancing a metre rule on a knife edge—become far more meaningful when students can test their predictions immediately. Together, these activities build intuition about forces, moments, and equilibrium, while also developing the careful reasoning needed for exam questions, where drawing the right free-body diagram often matters more than plugging numbers into equations.

4. Mop and back muscles

The following demonstration is a simulation of the enormous tension produced in the muscles of your back when you lean over. Tie a piece of string to the handle of a mop about a quarter of the way from the mop head. The head of the mop represents your head and the handle of the mop represents your spine. Drill a hole through the end of the handle furthest from the head and pivot it here, the head of the mop being at the top.

Now try and support the mop as it tilts by holding the string at a small angle to the handle of the mop (l am told that back muscles make an angle of only 10 0 with your spine!) The tension in the string represents the huge tension in your back muscles as you bend over. Bending at 450 produces a tension of over double your own body weight!

Holding something in your hands while you bend over into a car boot will increase the tension even further.

The tension in string (back muscles) T is given by the equation: Tsin A = mg cos 0 where A is the angle of the string with the mop (back muscles with the spine) and 0 is the angle that the mop handle (spine) makes with the horizontal. 5. Action men and artists models in Physics

The stability of the human body and the variable position of its centre of gravity can be studied using wooden artists’ models, action men or even Barbie dolls. This is an interesting example of everyday balancing. The trolleys are extremely stable and make a good example for the discussion of moments; the handles are almost directly above the back wheels. 7. Balances - wooden coat hangers

Wooden coat hangers make good simple balance arms. The hooks can be supported on a metal pivot and the design of the coat hanger gets the centre of mass of the system well below the pivot. Even wire ones will do if the masses that you are using for the balancing are not too great. They will probably need adjusting with a little plasticine first to get them to balance on their own.

8. Vectors and a rope

A very simple introduction to the importance of the directional nature of a force can be given by using a rope held horizontally and pulled tight by two strong pupils --- one pulling on either end. Now a relatively weak pupil should try to push down on the centre of the rope --- it is quite easy to move it down no matter how hard the other two pull.

Initially it is quite easy to push the rope downwards because of the directions of all the forces. The men are pulling horizontally and when the rope is pushed down only a very small fraction of their ‘pull’ is trying to prevent the rope from going downwards.

However, it gets harder and harder the further down the centre of the rope gets. This is because of the increased vertical component of the tension in the rope that must be counteracted by a larger downward force in the centre.

Theory:

Downward force = 2TsinA where T is the tension in the rope and A is the angle that the rope makes with the horizontal. Say T = 200 N and A = 100, i.e. a two metre rope pushed down in its centre by about 15 cm. This requires a downward force at the centre of only 34 N.

This is best done outside on the grass to avoid injury if the ‘pullers’ fall over.

Age range: 16 --- 18

Apparatus required: •Three students •About three metres of rope •Newton meter as an extension

12. Centre of gravity of a pupil

You can find the centre of gravity of a pupil by the following method using a strong wooden plank, some bathroom scales and a brick (or block of wood) the same height as the scales.

Put the plank down with one end on the block of wood or a brick and the other on the bathroom scales. Ask a pupil to lie on the plank with their heels over the pivot (brick). Record the reading of the scales. Take moments about the brick, having weighed the pupil first.

14. Moments

A very simple demonstration of the effect of distance on the turning effect of a force can be demonstrated using a door. Get a small pupil to close the door by pushing with one finger placed near the outer edge of the door while the teacher pushes with all their force on one hand very close to the hinge! The effect of the distance of a force from the pivot is clear - the pupil usually manages to push the door closed!

With older pupils, get one of them to push the door such that their arm is at an acute angle with the door. It is much harder to open or close it. This emphasises the importance of the words “perpendicular distance” in the definition of the moment of a force.

Age range: 11-13 or 16-18 depending on treatment Apparatus required: •Pupil and door

15. Using the centre of gravity to find the mass of a broom

This simple experiment emphasises that it is not just the mass on either side of a balance point that determines whether an object will be balanced, but also how it is distributed.

Hang up a broom by a string from roughly the centre of its handle so that the head will go down - the centre of mass of the broom being on the side nearest the broom head.

Now, by loading the other end with masses (m) bring the broom into a horizontal position. Locate the centre of gravity of the broom by removing the masses and adjusting the position of the string so that the broom balances.

Theory:

Force of weights x Distance of weights from pivot = Weight of broom x distance of centre of mass from pivot

Age range: 13-15

Apparatus required: •Retort stand and clamp •String •Broom •Slotted masses

16. The wooden spoon

Get a wooden spoon. Balance it on your finger to find the centre of mass. Mark this point and then cut the spoon into two pieces by cutting through the centre of mass. Fix a small piece of dowel rod so that the pieces can be joined together again. This makes a very instructive demonstration when you ask pupils which side of the spoon has the greatest mass --- they invariably say that both pieces are the same since they balance at the centre of mass.

Prove that they are not by measuring their mass with a balance.

It is the moment of the forces on either side of the pivot that must balance so the ‘bowl’ of the spoon must be heavier than the handle because its centre of mass is closer to the pivot point on which the whole spoon was originally balanced.

17. Rolling up hill

An interesting demonstration of the turning effect of a force is to make a tin roll up hill! Use a large, flattish cake or biscuit tin (one with a diameter of 30 cm and depth of 8 cm or so works well) and fix a hidden mass (such as a lump of plasticine) inside one rim and then show the pupils that the tin can rolls up hill. Of course you will start it so that the mass is slightly to the uphill side of the vertical, and the tin will only roll up hill until the mass reaches the lowest point - hence the advantage of a fairly large tin. See how long it takes them to spot what is going on without getting hold of the tin!

An alternative method is to use two large discs cut from thick sheets of polystyrene. A depression is made near their rims and a lump of plasticine wedged in it. The two discs are then stuck together - the loaded section being invisible!

18. Moving fingers on a long ruler

This is a surprising demonstration of the effect of frictional forces and balancing. Balance a metre ruler or a broom handle on two fingers, the fingers being at different distances from the two ends. Then slide your fingers together - they always meet in the centre of the ruler as long as it is uniform - this is good for a discussion of moments.

Of course, if the rod is not uniform, such as a snooker cue, the two fingers will meet at the centre of mass. (See the example about the polar bear on ice!) It may work better with two round pencils --- one held in each hand.

Theory:

The greater the distance of a finger from the centre of mass of the ruler, the smaller the reaction on it, and so the smaller the frictional force. The ruler will then slide more easily over this finger than the other, which is nearer the centre of mass. This situation continues until they are at equal distances, when both fingers move together.

Now try it with a 250g mass taped to one point of the ruler. This will move the centre of gravity of the ruler.

Apparatus required: •Metre ruler or other long uniform rod oTwo round pencils

19. Pile of leaning blocks!

This demonstration looks at stability. Take some rectangular blocks, dominoes are ideal, and pile one on top of the other, setting each domino a little off centre compared with the one below it. How many can you pile up, and where is the centre of mass of the whole stack?

Theory

A mathematical rule can be found to give the greatest overhang for a given number of blocks, each of length L. It can be shown that the N th block can have a maximum projection of L/(2N) relative to the block below. In other words the top, or first block can hang % its length over the second block, while the second block can hang % of its length over the third and so on. For a large number (N) of blocks the sum of the series is 0.5(0.5772 + In N).

Age range: 11-18 depending on treatment

Moments using a CD and magnets

This is a delightfully simple way of demonstrating the law of moments and how the turning effect depends on the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

Take a CD and fix a grid scale to it as shown in the diagram. You will then need four small button magnets. These should be fitted in pairs either side of the CD and equal distances from the centre. Mount the CD and magnets on a horizontal axle (such as a glass rod or pencil) so that it balances.

Now move one of the pairs of magnets vertically downwards. Although the distance of the magnets from the axle has increased, the perpendicular distance from the axle to the line of action of the weight of the magnets is unchanged and the CD remains balanced.

Age range: 11-16

23. The rolling spool

Take a cardboard cable spool about 40 cm in diameter similar to that shown in the photographs and fix a length of string to the central shaft.

First, set it up so that the string comes off the TOP of the shaft and try pulling the string. As you would expect you pull the spool towards you.

Now arrange it so that the string comes off the BOTTOM of the shaft. It may surprise you to find that the spool still rolls towards you! In both the above cases the string must be pulled horizontally. The critical point is when the line of action of the force in the string passes through the line where the two sides of the spool touch the table.

You may be able to prove this by considering moments about that point.

Age range: 16-19

Apparatus required: •Cable spool •String •Adhesive tape
