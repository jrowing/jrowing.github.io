Look at any pop-culture reference to radioactivity and you’ll see the same misconception repeated and reinforced - the eerie green-glow of “radioactive stuff”:

The idea that radioactive materials glow an eerie, toxic green is one of the most persistent scientific myths in modern culture. It feels intuitive, almost obvious, yet it is largely wrong. Radiation itself is invisible, colourless, and silent. The familiar green glow comes not from radiation as such, but from a complex mix of early scientific practices, tragic industrial history, optical effects, and decades of storytelling shorthand in film, television, and games.

The roots of the myth lie in the early twentieth century, when radioactivity was still new, poorly understood, and widely believed to be not just harmless but beneficial.

Radium, discovered by Marie and Pierre Curie in 1898, quickly became a wonder substance. It was marketed as invigorating and health-giving, added to cosmetics, drinking water, toothpaste, and—most famously—luminous paint. When radium was mixed with phosphors such as zinc sulfide, the radiation caused the phosphor to emit a soft greenish light. This glow did not require charging from sunlight and could last for years, making it ideal for watch dials, instrument panels, and aircraft controls.

This is where the association between radioactivity and green light truly began. The glow was real, visible, and commercially useful. But crucially, it was not the radiation, or the radioactive material itself that glowed. The radiation emitted merely excited another material, which then emitted visible light. That distinction was not widely understood at the time, and for the public, the glowing paint and the radioactive element became inseparable.

The human cost of this misunderstanding is most powerfully captured in the story of the Radium Girls. In factories across the United States during the 1910s and 1920s, young women were employed to paint watch dials with radium-based paint. To keep their brushes sharp, they were instructed to lick the tips between strokes—a practice known as “lip-pointing.” They were assured that the paint was safe. Some even painted their nails or teeth for fun, glowing faintly in the dark as a novelty.

The consequences were horrific. Radium behaves chemically like calcium, so when ingested it accumulates in bones. The women developed severe anemia, bone fractures, jaw necrosis, and cancers. Many died slow and painful deaths. Their struggle for recognition and compensation became a landmark case in occupational health law, helping to establish the principle that employers are responsible for workplace safety.

Kate Moore’s The Radium Girls tells this story with extraordinary clarity and compassion. It weaves together the science, the corporate negligence, and the personal tragedies in a way that makes the era feel immediate and real. It is not only a history of radiation, but of how scientific ignorance, profit, and gendered labour combined with devastating consequences. Anyone interested in the cultural mythology of radiation should read it, because it explains why glowing radium became such a powerful symbol—and why it came at such a terrible cost.

As nuclear physics developed, another kind of glow entered the public imagination: Cherenkov radiation.

This is the blue light seen in nuclear reactors submerged in water, caused when charged particles travel faster than light can move through that medium. It is a real, physical phenomenon, and it is strikingly beautiful—but it is blue, not green. However, it is rarely seen outside controlled environments, and it lacks the lurid, toxic aesthetic that fiction tends to favour.

Meanwhile, popular culture was busy solidifying the colour code. From mid-20th-century science fiction onward, green became shorthand for danger, mutation, and contamination. Comic books, B-movies, and later video games used glowing green liquids to signal radiation instantly and visually. It required no explanation. Audiences understood it intuitively. By the time franchises like The Simpsons, Fallout, and countless superhero films came along, the association was fixed. Green meant radioactive in the same way red means hot or skulls mean poison.

Popular in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Uranium glass added another layer to the myth - it contains small amounts of uranium salts and glows bright green under ultraviolet light.

Again, the glow is caused by fluorescence, not radiation, but to the eye it reinforces the idea that radioactive materials naturally shine green. Museums and collectors often display these objects under UV lamps today, further cementing the visual link.

What makes this misconception so persistent is that it blends just enough truth with a great deal of simplification. Yes, some radioactive materials were once used in glowing paints. Yes, radiation can produce visible light under certain conditions. But in everyday reality, radioactive substances look completely ordinary. Uranium metal resembles dull silver. Nuclear fuel rods are dark and solid. Radiation itself cannot be seen, smelled, or felt.

In that sense, the green glow is less a scientific fact and more a cultural symbol—a visual language we have collectively agreed upon to represent invisible danger. It is a reminder of how science becomes mythology, how real tragedies can be transformed into aesthetic tropes, and how powerful images can outlive the facts that created them.

Understanding where the myth comes from does more than correct a misconception. It opens the door to deeper conversations about radiation safety, scientific responsibility, and how society responds to new technologies. And, through the story of the Radium Girls, it reminds us that behind every glowing symbol in science fiction, there may be very real people who paid the price for our learning.

Ironically, in one of the biggest cases of it’s kind, were this misconception true, it would have helped a lot.

In 2013, the UK-based online retailer ASOS was forced to withdraw and recall a batch of leather belts after they were found to contain radioactive material. The issue came to light when one of the belts was flagged during routine screening in the United States. Tests revealed that the metal studs on the belt contained Cobalt-60, a man-made radioactive isotope commonly used in medical radiotherapy and industrial imaging.

Cobalt-60 does not occur naturally in consumer goods. Its presence strongly suggested that scrap metal contaminated with a radioactive source had entered the manufacturing supply chain, most likely during recycling. When metal from different sources is melted down together, so-called “orphan sources” — lost or improperly disposed radioactive materials — can be unknowingly incorporated into finished products. This is a known but rare problem in global metal recycling.

Once the contamination was confirmed, ASOS acted quickly. The belts were withdrawn from sale worldwide, and customers who had purchased them were contacted directly. Reports indicated that around 50 belts had already been sold across more than a dozen countries before the issue was identified. The company stressed that the health risk was low, but the presence of radioactive material alone was enough to justify a full recall.

What makes this case especially interesting is how it contrasts with public fears about radiation. Here, there was no glow, no visible warning, and no dramatic sign of danger. The belts looked completely ordinary. They were only discovered because of radiation screening equipment, not because anyone noticed something unusual. This is the opposite of how radiation is usually imagined in popular culture, where danger is associated with glowing green substances or visible emissions…
