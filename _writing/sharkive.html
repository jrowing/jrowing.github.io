The Sharkive

A citizen-science record of the sharks landed and sold at markets and roadside stalls in Seychelles: why it exists, how it works, and where it could go next.

Open The Sharkive →

What it is

The Sharkive is a phone web app for photographing dead sharks at fish markets and roadside stalls. Anyone can log one, with no account and no personal details. Every submission is checked by a reviewer, and confirmed records are published with the species, a date, time and location, and a downloadable data file.

The aim is a long-term, consistent record that could one day show which species are being landed, how often threatened species turn up, where sharks are sold around the islands, and whether any of that is changing over seasons and years.

Why it matters

Sharks grow slowly, mature late and have few young, so populations that decline recover slowly. Many of the species in the western Indian Ocean are listed as threatened on the IUCN Red List.

The sharks you see on a market table or at the side of the road mostly come from small-scale, artisanal fishing, and those catches are rarely recorded in any detail. Without that information it is hard to say which species are being caught, how big they are, or whether things are getting better or worse. A photograph taken by someone who happens to be passing is a small piece of evidence; thousands of them, collected the same way over years, start to become data.

How it works

  • Photograph the shark. Use the in-app camera or an original photo from the camera roll.
  • When and where come from the photo, never from the user. Capture time comes from the shutter press or the photo’s EXIF data, and location from GPS at the shutter or the EXIF GPS. Photos without a trustworthy capture time are refused, and once a record is saved its time and location can’t be edited by anyone, reviewers included. Each record also stores where its time and location came from, so analysis can filter by data quality.
  • A species suggestion, made on the phone. The photo is turned into an image fingerprint and compared with reviewer-confirmed photos. It is a matching tool, not generative AI, it works offline, and it improves with every confirmed record.
  • Human review. Every record waits in a queue until a reviewer confirms the species or rejects it. Approved photos join the reference library.
  • Open records. Confirmed records are published with species, family, IUCN status, time, place and a map, and can be exported as CSV.

Under the hood it is a static site with a Supabase database. The code is open on GitHub.

About sharks, not people

The Sharkive records what is caught, not who caught or sold it. It doesn’t collect the names of fishers or vendors, and it isn’t an enforcement tool. Fishers and vendors know more about sharks than almost anyone, and a record like this only works with their goodwill. Contributors are asked to frame the shark rather than people, and to ask before photographing someone’s stall or catch.

Where it is now

The app is live and taking records. It is run independently from Praslin: I build and host it, review submissions, and build up the reference library that the identifier learns from.

What next

Long-term data needs long-term people, so in time The Sharkive may work alongside, or be handed over to, a local wildlife or marine conservation organisation. It is built to make that easy: static files, a documented database and exportable records. Whoever runs it, the records stay open and it stays about sharks, not people.

If you live in or visit Seychelles and see a shark for sale, log it.
