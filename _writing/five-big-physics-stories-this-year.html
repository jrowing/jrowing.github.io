If you follow physics news closely, this year probably looked like a blur of headlines: new particles hinted at, sharper images of the universe, ever-larger simulations. If you teach physics, though, the year looks rather different.

I try not to teach physics news. But big stories can be useful, if you treat them as lenses rather than content.

1. Ever better images of the universe

From space telescopes to gravitational-wave detections, we’re seeing the universe in more detail than ever.

  • New James Webb Space Telescope image (galaxy collision) — stunning visuals for discussing resolution, noise, and indirect evidence. Live Science

  • GW250114 — clearest gravitational-wave signal yet — great for explaining signal-to-noise and inference from indirect data. Wikipedia

  • Gravitational waves may reveal dark matter — shows how theory + data interplay. ScienceDaily

In the classroom: these are really a story about measurement. Resolution, noise, signal processing, indirect evidence. A perfect way to remind students that physics isn’t about “seeing” things directly — it’s about inferring reality from imperfect data.

Activity ideas

1. “What can you really see?”

  • Show students:

    • a blurred JWST image

    • the processed final image

  • Ask:

    • What’s measured?

    • What’s inferred?

    • What assumptions are baked into the image?

  • Follow-up: compare with how we infer atomic structure or electric fields.

2. Signal vs noise demo

  • Give students a noisy dataset (or a blurred image).

  • Ask them to decide:

    • Is there a signal?

    • How confident are you?

  • Tie directly to gravitational wave detection.

3. Resolution thought experiment

  • “If your telescope had half the resolution, what disappears first?”

  • Links nicely to:

    • diffraction

    • wavelength

    • uncertainty

Key takeaway:
Physics isn’t about seeing — it’s about extracting meaning from imperfect measurements.

2. Progress towards fusion energy

Another year of headlines suggesting we’re “closer than ever”.

  • Fusion trends to watch in 2025 (IAEA World Fusion Outlook) — overview of global developments toward fusion energy. IAEA

  • Record-breaking fusion progress around the world (Scientific American) — experimental advances in devices like Wendelstein 7-X and JET. Scientific American

  • Fusion funding and commercialization momentum — shows why fusion is back in the headlines. cfs.energy

In the classroom: an opportunity to talk about energy accounting. Where does the energy actually come from? What counts as “net gain”? It’s an excellent antidote to vague ideas about energy being created or used up.

Activity ideas

1. Energy flow diagram
Students draw:

  • Energy in → plasma heating

  • Energy lost → radiation, inefficiencies

  • Energy captured → electricity

Then ask:

At what point would this count as ‘net gain’?

This exposes confusion between:

  • energy produced

  • energy captured

  • energy usable

2. Fusion headline critique
Give them a real headline:

“Fusion breakthrough brings limitless energy closer”

Ask:

  • What’s missing from this statement?

  • What numbers would you want?

  • What does “breakthrough” actually mean?

3. Compare fusion vs fission
Have them build a table.

3. The Standard Model still stubbornly holding together

Hints of new physics, followed by careful null results.

  • Why the Standard Model hasn’t actually cracked — a clear, year-end article about how widely tested physics still fits current data. Big Think

  • ATLAS finds evidence for rare Higgs decay — a precise Standard Model test and great classroom case study on how experiments limit new physics. physics.ox.ac.uk

In the classroom: this is about models and limits. A model doesn’t stop being useful just because it isn’t complete. Students often think a theory is either “true” or “wrong”; modern physics shows how much happens in the grey area.

Activity ideas

1. “Is this model wrong?” debate
Give students statements like:

  • Newton’s laws are wrong.

  • The Standard Model is incomplete.

  • Models must perfectly match reality.

They must argue for or against each.

This reframes physics as useful approximations, not truths.

2. Prediction vs explanation
Show a historical anomaly (e.g. Mercury’s orbit).
Ask:

  • Did Newton’s laws stop working?

  • Or did we find a better model?

3. Model hierarchy task
Students rank:

  • Newtonian mechanics

  • Special relativity

  • General relativity

According to:

  • simplicity

  • accuracy

  • usefulness

Excellent for conceptual depth.

4. AI and simulation everywhere in physics

From climate models to particle physics.

  • AI boosting gravitational-wave detection — AI helping real physics measurement, not just simulation. GeekWire

In the classroom: a chance to separate calculation from understanding. Just because a computer can produce an answer doesn’t mean we understand the mechanism. A useful parallel with students relying too heavily on formulas without physical meaning.

Activity ideas

1. “Does the computer understand?”
Show an AI-generated result or graph.
Ask:

  • What inputs did it need?

  • What assumptions are hidden?

  • Could it explain why the result occurs?

2. Black box vs white box
Give students:

  • A formula they understand

  • A simulation they don’t

Ask which one they trust more — and why.

3. Error hunting
Give a simulated dataset with:

  • one unrealistic assumption

  • one hidden bias

Ask students to identify what might go wrong.

This is brilliant for:

  • data literacy

  • scientific skepticism

  • exam technique

5. Extreme physics becoming routine

Black holes, neutron stars, ultra-strong fields — once exotic, now mainstream.

  • Black hole mergers and tests of fundamental physics — rigorous confirmation of a Hawking-area law prediction and how gravitational waves test theory. news.northwestern.edu

  • New cosmic event “superkilonova” observations — neutron star and massive star aftermaths showing multimessenger astrophysics at work. Space

In the classroom: this helps reinforce the idea that the same laws apply everywhere. Newton’s laws don’t stop working in space; they just need to be applied carefully. A powerful way to counter the idea that “real” physics only happens in labs or textbooks.

Activity ideas

1. “Would Newton still work here?”
Give scenarios:

  • Near a black hole

  • On a neutron star

  • Inside a particle accelerator

Ask:

  • Which laws still apply?

  • Which need modification?

  • Which approximations fail?

2. Scale comparison
Have students compare:

  • gravitational force between two people

  • between Earth and Moon

  • inside a black hole merger

They quickly see why extremes matter.

3. Thought experiment: falling into a black hole
Ask:

  • What would you feel?

  • What would an outside observer see?

  • Which physics ideas break first?

Great for reinforcing reference frames.

What I’ve enjoyed most this year is leaning into explanation rather than correction. Not saying that’s wrong, but let’s follow that idea and see where it breaks. Physics becomes much more human when you treat misconceptions as stepping stones rather than failures.

Physics isn’t hard because the maths is difficult. It’s hard because it asks you to let go of intuitive pictures and replace them with better ones — pictures that don’t always feel right, but are right.

A Unifying Classroom Question

You can tie all five together with one question:

“What does physics actually do — describe reality, predict outcomes, or explain mechanisms?”

Every topic this year feeds into that.
