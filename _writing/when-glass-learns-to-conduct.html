At room temperature, glass is the textbook insulator. You can put it in series with an LED and a 15 V supply and nothing happens. No glow, no current, no drama.

Then you heat it.

As the Bunsen flame licks the glass and it approaches its softening point, something remarkable happens: the LED flickers, then glows steadily. The glass—still recognisably glass—has begun to conduct.

It’s one of those rare demonstrations where nothing has changed and yet everything has changed.

The Setup: Deceptively Simple

The circuit could hardly be more ordinary:

  • A DC supply (around 15 V)

  • An LED (with appropriate current limiting)

  • A strip or rod of glass completing the circuit, here I use a cheap test-tube with un-insulated copper wire wrapped round it.

At first, the glass behaves exactly as students expect. The LED stays dark. Predictions are confident.

The moment the flame is applied, expectations begin to wobble.

The Physics Story: Insulators Aren’t Forever

From a physics perspective, the demo confronts a deeply embedded idea: that materials belong in neat boxes labelled “conductor” and “insulator”.

Heating the glass adds energy to the system. As the temperature rises:

  • Ions within the glass gain mobility

  • Electrical resistance drops dramatically

  • Charge transport becomes possible

This is not metallic conduction. There is no sea of free electrons. Instead, the current is carried by moving ions, and that distinction matters.

Students can be guided to think about:

  • How resistance depends on temperature

  • Why this behaviour is the opposite of metals

  • What “charge carrier” really means

It’s an elegant way to introduce the idea that conduction mechanisms differ across materials—even when the macroscopic effect (current flowing) looks the same.

The Chemistry Story: A Solid That Isn’t Quite Solid

Chemistry explains why heating works at all.

Glass isn’t a perfect crystal; it’s an amorphous solid with ions locked into a rigid network. At high temperatures, that network loosens. Alkali ions—often sodium—are no longer fixed in place. They become mobile.

In other words, the glass hasn’t melted, but it has crossed a threshold where ionic movement becomes possible.

This opens the door to conversations about:

  • Ionic mobility in solids

  • The difference between melting and softening

  • Why glass behaves more like a supercooled liquid than a crystal

  • The relationship between structure and properties

The LED lighting is not just a physics result; it’s chemical structure revealing itself.

The Moment That Matters

Pedagogically, the most powerful part of the demo is the delay.

Nothing happens…
Nothing happens…
Then suddenly, light.

That pause forces students to confront the idea of thresholds. The glass doesn’t gradually “become a conductor” in any obvious way. Instead, it crosses a boundary where the microscopic behaviour changes enough to matter macroscopically.

This is a perfect moment to ask:

  • What changed inside the glass?

  • Why didn’t increasing the voltage have the same effect?

  • Would cooling reverse the process?

Where the Subjects Meet

You cannot fully explain this demo using only physics or only chemistry.

  • Physics describes the current, the resistance, and the energy transfer

  • Chemistry explains the structure, the ions, and their liberation

  • Materials science quietly sits in the middle, smiling

It’s a rare classroom moment where disciplinary borders dissolve naturally, not artificially.

Why This Demo Sticks

Students remember this one.
They remember that heat turned an insulator into a conductor.
And, crucially, they remember that properties depend on conditions.

In a curriculum full of definitions and categories, this demo does something more valuable: it destabilises certainty—and replaces it with understanding.

If you want a single demonstration that rewards curiosity, respects complexity, and genuinely links physics and chemistry, heating glass until it conducts is hard to beat.
