General theory for this section

Definition

  • Capacitance is the ability of a system to store electric charge per unit potential difference.

  • A capacitor consists of two conductive plates separated by an insulator (dielectric).

Capacitance is defined as:

\[C = \frac{Q}{V}\]

Where:

  • C is capacitance (in farads, F)

  • Q is charge stored (in coulombs, C)

  • V is potential difference (in volts, V)

Units

  • Typical capacitors are in F, nF, or pF due to large unit size.

Energy Stored in a Capacitor

A charged capacitor stores energy in the electric field between the plates:

\[E = \frac{1}{2} QV = \frac{1}{2} CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}\]

Capacitance of a Parallel Plate Capacitor

For a parallel plate capacitor:

\[C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r A}{d}\]

Where:

  • Epsilon 0 = permittivity of free space ()

  • Epsilon r = relative permittivity (dimensionless)

  • A = area of the plates (m²)

  • d = separation between the plates (m)

Combining Capacitors

In Parallel:

  • Total capacitance adds, think about the area increasing:

    \[C_{\text{total}} = C_1 + C_2 + \dots\]

  • Same voltage across each capacitor.

In Series:

  • Reciprocal of total capacitance:

    \[\frac{1}{C_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots\]

  • Same charge on each capacitor.

Charging and Discharging (RC Circuits)

For a capacitor in a circuit with resistance :

Charging:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Voltage across capacitor: }\\ V(t) &= V_0 \left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)\\ \text{Charge on the plates: }\\ Q(t) &= Q_0 \left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)\\ \text{Current in the circuit: }\\ I(t) &= \frac{V_0}{R} e^{-t/RC} \end{aligned}\]

Discharging:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Voltage and charge: }\\ V(t) &= V_0 e^{-t/RC},\\ \quad Q(t) &= Q_0 e^{-t/RC}\\ \\ \text{Current: }\\ I(t) &= -\frac{V_0}{R} e^{-t/RC} \end{aligned}\]

Time Constant

  • The time constant is given by:

    \[\tau = RC\]

  • It represents the time taken for voltage or charge to fall to approx 37% of its initial value (to 0.693 of initial value). In the same way as half-life in radioactivity Capacitors discharge exponentially, meaning they lose a fixed fraction of their remaining charge in each successive period.

Applications of Capacitors

  • Energy storage (e.g. camera flashes)

  • Timing circuits

  • Smoothing in power supplies

  • Filtering in AC circuits

Common Misconception 1

A very common misconception about capacitors, particularly at GCSE/IGCSE and A-level, is the idea that charge somehow flows through the capacitor or is used up inside it. Students often describe the charge on a capacitor as being “shared between the plates”, or imagine that charge passes across the gap in the same way that it flows through a resistor. From this, several related misunderstandings follow. Learners may believe that the capacitor stores charge inside it, that one plate ends up with more charge because charge has been lost elsewhere in the circuit, or that current stops flowing simply because there is “no charge left to move”.

In reality, charge does not flow through the capacitor at all. The dielectric between the plates is an insulator, so no electrons can cross the gap. Any current that flows during charging exists only in the external circuit. What actually happens inside the capacitor is not charge flow but charge separation: electrons are rearranged so that one plate becomes negatively charged and the other positively charged, without any charge crossing the dielectric.

It is also important to be clear about what a capacitor stores. A capacitor does not store charge in the sense of accumulating a net amount of it. Taken together, the two plates always have zero total charge. Instead, a capacitor stores energy in the electric field that exists between the plates when they are charged. The energy is associated with the separation of charge and the resulting electric field, not with charge being “held” inside the device.

As a capacitor charges, equal and opposite charges appear on its plates. One plate acquires a charge of +Q, while the other acquires a charge of −Q. These charges are equal in magnitude, opposite in sign, and are supplied by the power source rather than created by the capacitor itself. This is why the familiar relation $Q = CV$ refers to the magnitude of the charge on one plate, not the total charge of the capacitor.

When a capacitor is connected to a battery, electrons are pushed onto one plate and pulled off the other. This movement of charge takes place around the circuit, through the wires and the battery, rather than across the gap between the plates. As charge builds up on the plates, the potential difference across the capacitor increases. The growing electric field between the plates increasingly opposes further charge movement, so the current in the circuit gradually decreases and eventually falls to zero.

A common explanation students give for this behaviour is that the capacitor becomes “full”. A more accurate explanation is that current stops because the potential difference across the capacitor has risen to equal the battery voltage. At that point, there is no longer a net driving force to push charge around the circuit, so the current ceases even though plenty of mobile charge carriers remain in the wires.

A helpful analogy is to imagine two rigid water tanks separated by a flexible membrane. A pump forces water into one tank while removing water from the other. No water crosses the membrane, but the membrane becomes stretched and stores energy. In the same way, charge does not cross the dielectric of a capacitor; instead, energy is stored as a result of charge separation. The key idea to emphasise is that capacitors are about separation and fields, not about storing charge inside.

When a charged capacitor is connected to an uncharged capacitor…

This common A-level question causes a number of very persistent misconceptions to surface. This makes the situation particularly valuable for conceptual probing, especially at A-level, because it tests students’ understanding of charge, voltage and energy rather than their ability to apply formulas.

One common misconception is the belief that the charge simply splits equally between the two capacitors. Students often assume that half of the original charge ends up on each capacitor, reasoning that the charge is being “shared” and therefore must divide evenly. This idea is only correct in the special case where the two capacitors have equal capacitance. In reality, total charge is conserved, so the sum of the final charges on the two capacitors equals the initial charge, but how that charge divides depends entirely on the capacitances involved, not on any notion of fairness or equal sharing.

This partners with a particularly strong misconception - that the voltage on the initially charged capacitor remains unchanged. Students often think that the charged capacitor somehow “keeps its voltage” while the uncharged capacitor merely takes some charge from it. However, when capacitors are connected in parallel, the voltage across them must equalise. In the final state, both capacitors have the same potential difference across them, regardless of their initial conditions.

Even more challenging is the assumption that energy is conserved in this process. Many students, including high-attaining ones, expect the total electrical energy stored before and after the connection to be the same. In fact, electrical energy is not conserved when a charged capacitor is connected to an uncharged one. Some of the initial energy is dissipated as thermal energy in the connecting wires and some is radiated away as electromagnetic waves. This remains true even in the idealised case where the wires are assumed to have negligible resistance.

The correct conceptual sequence (parallel connection)

Assume:

  • Capacitor C1 charged to V0​

  • Capacitor C2 initially uncharged

  • Connected plate-to-plate

Step-by-step

  1. A p.d. exists between corresponding plates → current flows

  2. Charge redistributes around the circuit

  3. Voltages equalise:

    \[V_f=\frac{Q_{initial}}{C_1+C_2}​​\]

  4. Final charges:

    \[Q_1​=C_1​V_f​, \quad \quad Q_2=C_2​V_f​,\]

Energy misconception made explicit

Initial energy:

\[E_i = \frac{1}{2} C_1 V_{0}^{2}\]

Final energy:

\[\begin{align} E_f &= \frac{1}{2} (C_{1} + C_{2})V_{f}^{2}\\ \text{since:} \quad \quad\\ V_{f} &<V_{0}\\ \\ \therefore \quad E_{f} &<E_{i} \end{align}\]

Charge is conserved; energy is not.

Diagnostic questions that reveal misconceptions

  • “Does charge cross the dielectric?”

  • “What must be equal when capacitors are connected in parallel: charge or voltage?”

  • “Where did the missing energy go?”

  • “If the wire had zero resistance, would energy still be lost?”

Stories to enrich

The Shocking Leyden Jar (1745) – The First Capacitor

In the 18th century, Pieter van Musschenbroek of Leyden accidentally invented one of the first capacitors: the Leyden jar. He was experimenting with static electricity and stored charge in a glass jar lined with metal foil and filled with water.

When he touched the wire leading into the jar, he received a massive shock — so intense, he wrote that he “would not take a second shock for the kingdom of France.”

This early capacitor could store and release electric charge — and it amazed scientists across Europe. It even became a party trick in salons!

Benjamin Franklin’s Electric Kiss

Ben Franklin, fascinated by Leyden jars, used them in all kinds of experiments. In one infamous case, he proposed a romantic (and dangerous) experiment where a man and a woman stood on insulating blocks and exchanged a charged Leyden jar by kissing.

It worked. The shock delivered through the kiss demonstrated how electric potential builds up and discharges — an early (and painful) demonstration of how capacitors release energy.

The Camera Flash

Have you ever heard the high-pitched whine of an old camera charging before a flash? That sound is the capacitor charging up.

When you press the shutter, the capacitor discharges all its energy in an instant to power the bright flash of light. It’s a perfect example of how capacitors can store energy and release it very quickly — useful in real-world tech.

Supercapacitors in Modern Cars

Modern electric vehicles (like Teslas or buses in China) use supercapacitors to assist with regenerative braking. When the car brakes, kinetic energy is converted into electrical energy and stored instantly in a supercapacitor.

Unlike batteries, which take time to charge and degrade over time, supercapacitors can charge and discharge very quickly and survive millions of cycles.

This technology helps improve energy efficiency and reduce wear on batteries.

Capacitor Failures: The “Capacitor Plague” (2000s)

In the early 2000s, millions of computers and electronics failed due to faulty capacitors made with stolen — but incorrect — electrolyte formulas.

The capacitors would bulge, leak, or explode, taking motherboards and power supplies with them. Entire product lines were ruined, and manufacturers faced massive recalls.

A small component — the humble capacitor — caused a major global electronics scandal.

The smoothing action of a capacitor

A capacitor placed across the output of a rectifier unit will give a smoothing effect to the output. An analogy of this is to use a balloon to smooth the flow of water. Fix a tube to a water tap, and into this fit a T piece with a balloon hanging down from one branch of the T.

As water flows through the tube the balloon will distend. As the flow decreases, the balloon will shrink - maintaining the output flow. If the flow increases the balloon expands, smoothing out the flow, and keeping the flow rate approximately constant. The expansion and contraction of the balloon is analogous to the charging and discharging of the capacitor.
