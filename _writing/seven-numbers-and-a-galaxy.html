There is an equation I love teaching almost more than any other, and it is barely an equation at all. It’s not in the spec, it has no derivation and it predicts nothing you can check. More than that, any honest physicist will tell you, that several of its terms are essentially unknowable. And yet it is one of the most quietly profound things you can put in front of a student, because it takes a huge and exciting question — are we alone? — and turns it into something you can actually reason about.

It is the Drake equation, and I have built a calculator for it that you can play with. It lives here:

jrowing.com/resources/drake.html

Open it in another tab, have a slide of the sliders, and come back. The rest of this post is about what you are doing when you move them — which turns out to be a part of the philosophy and nature of physics itself.

What the equation says

In 1961, the astronomer Frank Drake was preparing for the first serious scientific meeting on the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. He wanted a way to organise the conversation — to take the impossibly vague question how many civilisations might we be able to detect? and break it into pieces small enough to argue about one at a time.

What he wrote down was this:

\[N = R* × f_p × n_e × f_l × f_i × f_c × L\]

Seven factors, multiplied together, giving N: the number of civilisations in our galaxy whose signals we might, in principle, detect. Each factor is a separate link in a chain that runs from raw astronomy up to technology:

  • \[\begin{align} R* &— \text{ the rate at which new stars form in the Milky Way.}\\ f_p &—\text{ the fraction of those stars that have planets.}\\ n_e &—\text{ the average number of potentially habitable planets per system.}\\ f_l &—\text{ the fraction of habitable planets where life actually arises.}\\ f_i &—\text{ the fraction of those where life becomes intelligent.}\\ f_c &—\text{ the fraction of intelligent species that develop detectable technology.}\\ L &—\text{ the length of time such a civilisation keeps broadcasting.} \end{align}\]

Like most fermi-problems, the genius of it is the structure. You do not have to answer are we alone? in one heroic leap. You answer seven smaller questions, each in its own field — galactic astronomy, then planetary science, then biology, then evolution, then sociology — and let the multiplication carry you from one end to the other. It is a machine for converting a question nobody can answer into seven questions some of which we can.

The numbers are not created equal…

Move the first slider — the star formation rate — and you are nudging a number we actually know. Infrared and H-alpha surveys of the galaxy’s star-forming regions put it at something like one to three solar masses of new stars per year. That is a measured quantity. We are not guessing.

Move the second and third sliders — the fraction of stars with planets, and the number of habitable worlds per system — and you are standing on ground that has firmed up dramatically in your own lifetime. When Drake first wrote the equation, this was a near-total unknown. We now know that planets are not rare accidents but the ordinary furniture of the galaxy; very nearly every star has them. A term that was once philosophy is now, increasingly, data. If you teach this equation, that shift is worth dwelling on: a slider that your own teachers would have had to wildly guess at, you can now set from a survey of thousands of real worlds.

And then you reach the fourth slider, and the floor drops away.

The fraction of habitable planets where life actually begins. The fraction of those where it becomes intelligent. The fraction of intelligent species that build a radio telescope. For every one of these, we have exactly one data point. Earth. We are the only example of life, of intelligence, and of technology that we have ever found, and you cannot do statistics with a sample size of one. Complex intelligence arose precisely once in the history of the only biosphere we know, which makes any value you choose a confession of belief rather than a measurement.

That is why the calculator shows you the source of every term as you touch it. I wanted it to be impossible to move a slider without being told how much you are allowed to trust it. Some of these numbers are reports from the frontier of observational astronomy. Others are barely more than a number you have decided to feel comfortable with. The equation puts them side by side and multiplies them together as if they were equals, and they are absolutely not equals, and learning to feel that difference in your hands is the whole point.

This is a Fermi problem wearing its best clothes

Regular readers will know I have a soft spot for Fermi problems — the art of estimating something to within an order of magnitude from almost no information. How many piano tuners in Chicago; how many blades of grass on the school field; how much does a cloud weigh. The trick is always the same: break the impossible question into a chain of guessable ones, be honest about the uncertainty in each, and multiply.

The Drake equation is the most famous Fermi problem ever written down. It is exactly that move — decompose, estimate, multiply — applied to the grandest possible target. Drake was not claiming to know how many civilisations are out there. He was doing on a galactic scale, precisely what we ask a sixteen-year-old to do with the piano tuners: making the unknowable tractable by chopping it into parts, and being disciplined about which parts are solid and which are sand.

And the multiplication does something brutal and instructive. Because the terms are multiplied, not added, a single tiny factor can annihilate the whole product. You can be wildly generous with the first six sliders — billions of stars, planets everywhere, life springing up readily — and then set the intelligence fraction to one in a million, and watch N collapse toward zero. Conversely, a hopeful nudge on the civilisation lifetime L can resurrect it. The estimate is hostage to its weakest, least-known link. That is true of every Fermi estimate, but here, where the weak links are whether life is rare and whether intelligence is a fluke, the lesson lands with real weight: our best guess about whether we have company in the universe depends almost entirely on the two or three numbers we understand least.

When is a model good enough?

Underneath all of this sits the question I think is actually at the core of physics, far more than any particular equation: when is a model good enough?

Students often arrive imagining that physics is a collection of true statements about the world. It is not. It is a collection of models — deliberate simplifications, each one good enough for some purpose and useless for others. We model a falling ball as a point mass and ignore the air, and that is good enough to predict where it lands, and not good enough to design a parachute. We model a gas as tiny perfectly-elastic spheres, and that is good enough for the ideal gas law and hopeless near a phase change. The skill we are really teaching, underneath the content, is the judgement of which simplification is good enough for the question in front of you.

The Drake equation is a model whose honesty about its own limits is unusually naked. Nobody who writes it down believes it gives you N. What it gives you is a framework — a way of putting bounds on your ignorance. Its output is not an answer; its output is a map of which questions matter most. If your estimate swings by a factor of a billion depending on the intelligence term, then the equation has told you something genuinely useful: it has told you that the future of this entire question lives in origin-of-life chemistry and evolutionary biology, not in building bigger radio dishes. The model is “good enough” not because it predicts the right number, but because it correctly identifies where our ignorance is most expensive.

That is a subtle and grown-up idea, and the equation is a wonderful vehicle for it. A model can be valuable precisely because it shows you, cleanly, what you do not know. A wrong number, honestly arrived at, can be worth more than a right number you cannot interrogate.

What I want students to take from it;

Three things, really.

The first is wonder. There is no slider on that calculator you can move without thinking about something genuinely vast — the rate at which stars are being born above your head right now, the billions of unseen worlds we have only just learned are there, the staggering improbability or inevitability (we do not know which) of a planet waking up and looking back at the sky. You cannot play with this honestly and stay unmoved. That, on its own, is worth the lesson.

The second is humility about numbers. We spend a great deal of time in physics teaching students to compute, and rather less teaching them to ask how much should I trust this number I have just computed? The Drake equation forces the question. Two of its terms are among the best-measured quantities in modern astronomy; two others are essentially educated wishes. The discipline of holding both in your head at once, and not pretending the wish is a measurement, is one of the most important habits a scientist can have. (I’ve a whole article in the works about scientists that have fallen foul of this).

The third is the thing about models. When is the model good enough? The answer is never “when it gives the right answer,” because for most interesting questions we do not know the right answer, and for the rest there’s always a factor not properly included. The answer is “when it lets you reason more clearly than you could without it.” By that standard, an equation that cannot predict a single thing, whose terms we mostly cannot measure, that has never once been tested against reality, is one of the best models we have. It has been organising humanity’s thinking about its own cosmic loneliness for over sixty years, and it does that job beautifully.

Go and move the sliders. Set them to what you genuinely believe and see what falls out. Then change the one you are least sure about and watch the galaxy empty or fill. Somewhere in that swing — between a cosmos teeming with company and one in which we are heartbreakingly alone — is an honest picture of how much we still have to learn.

That gap is not a failure of the equation. It is the equation. And it is, I think, one of the most beautiful things in all of science.
