There’s something quietly mesmerizing about watching molecules move — invisible particles wandering randomly, spreading from where there are many to where there are few. In the lab, we can make this invisible motion visible, and one of my new favorite demonstrations does exactly that using agar, thymolphthalein, and a dash of chemistry.

To make this work we produce a cube of agar, a gelatin-like substance commonly used to grow bacteria in a Petri dish, dosed with a little thymolphthalein, a pH indicator and some sodium hydroxide (0.1-0.5M). At neutral or slightly acidic pH, thymolphthalein is colorless, hiding in plain sight. But introduce a base, such as sodium hydroxide, and watch it turn a brilliant blue.

The beauty of this demonstration lies in its simplicity: the agar cube sits passively in a dish, and when a drop of Hydrochloric acid touches it (0.5m), the ions begin to migrate into the gel. From the outside edges inward, a clear front slowly spreads, revealing the otherwise invisible motion of molecules. This is diffusion in action — molecules moving from regions of high concentration to regions of low concentration, entirely on their own, no pumps or fans required.

What makes the agar cube demo so compelling, in my opinion, is its three-dimensionality. Unlike the classic Petri dish diffusion experiments, where substances spread across a flat surface, here we see diffusion penetrating a solid cube. The rate at which the blue spreads depends on factors you might expect: a stronger acid produces a faster color change, a smaller cube clears more quickly, and warmth speeds up the molecular motion. Even the density of the agar matters — a firmer gel slows the march of the blue front.

Technically, what we observe is both diffusion and chemistry at once. The acid ions diffuse into the cube, and as they move, they trigger a chemical reaction with the indicator, producing the visible blue color. But the star of the show is diffusion: passive, elegant, and entirely driven by the random motion of molecules.

For students, scientists, and the curious alike, the thymolphthalein agar cube is more than just a classroom trick. It’s a window into the invisible processes that govern life, chemistry, and the physical world around us. It’s a reminder that even in a small gel cube, there’s movement, change, and a story unfolding in slow, blue-tinged waves.

Recipe:

The original prac is in Biological Science, The Web of Life Teacher’s Guide Part 2, Exercise 10b.4

  1. Approx 25g agar

  2. just enough NaOH (0.1-0.5) to cause blue colour change approx 100ml

  3. Heat 900ml water in a beaker almost to boiling

  4. Add agar and continue boiling while stirring till dissolved.

  5. Allow to cool until temperature drops below 60C then add NaOH, still stirring.
    Add thymolphthalein until very deep blue – 6 droppers full.

  6. Allow to set then cut unto 1cm, 2cm and 3cm cubes, leaving some for cylinders.

  7. Can be made 2-3 days in advance if you take it out of container and leave loosely covered with plastic wrap on glass Petri dish and store in chemical store shelf.

Note that if you use insufficient NaOH the cubes will slowly clear as the damp agar acidifies from the CO2 in the room acidifying the surface. This effect is stronger before the gel is set.
