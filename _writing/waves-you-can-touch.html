There is a moment, fairly early in most waves units, when you can feel the class starting to drift. The diagrams are fine. The equations are fine. But somewhere around the third mention of wavelength, the physics stops feeling real.

This post is about getting it back.

What follows is not a comprehensive scheme of work. It’s a collection of the experiments and demonstrations I reach for when I want students to stop thinking about waves and start feeling them — in their fingertips, through their chairs, in the sudden catch of a note that rises then falls as a buzzer swings past their ears.

Most of what’s here costs very little. Some of it costs nothing at all.

Start with a skipping rope

Before you write a single equation, get two students standing at either end of a long rope or a length of Bunsen tubing and ask them to swing it. Tell them nothing. Let them discover, for themselves, that most frequencies simply don’t work. Something odd is happening: there are only certain rates of swinging that produce a stable pattern. Everything else collapses into chaos.

That’s the introduction to standing waves, and it required no apparatus at all.

When they’ve found the fundamental — one smooth arch — push them higher. Two arches. Three. The points of stillness (you haven’t used the word node yet) are obvious, physical, and a little uncanny. The rope wants to stand still at those points. Why?

The answer, of course, is destructive interference — but the students don’t need that word yet. What they need is the felt intuition that only certain patterns are stable, that the wave is doing something to itself, and that the mathematics is describing something genuinely strange and beautiful about how disturbances travel.

The electric toothbrush trick

Once students have found standing waves in the rope, you want to formalise it — and the standard vibration generator demonstration is entirely serviceable. But if your kit is incomplete or unreliable, here is a better option.

Remove the brush head from an electric toothbrush. Thread a length of light elastic from the metal vibrating rod, clamp the toothbrush in a retort stand, and hold the far end of the elastic in your hand. Switch it on. Adjust the tension.

The standing wave appears almost immediately, and adjusting tension changes the harmonic. Students can do this themselves, holding the elastic in their fingers, and they can feel the amplitude at the antinodes — a faint buzzing pull at the nodes, nothing at all.

It is, as demonstrations go, wonderfully cheap. A supermarket toothbrush costs less than a cup of coffee and will produce cleaner standing waves than many signal generators I’ve used.

On the Doppler effect: let it be visceral

Here is how I do the Doppler effect.

I tape a small piezoelectric buzzer to a length of string. The buzzer is connected via long wires to a battery I hold in my other hand. Then I swing the buzzer over my head — not tentatively, but properly, at arm’s length, at two revolutions a second — and I ask the class to raise their hands when the pitch is highest.

The effect is immediate and dramatic. The students on one side of the room hear something completely different from the students on the other side, and they will argue about it cheerfully. The person doing the swinging, they discover, hears nothing shift at all. Why not?

That question — *why does it only work for the observer, not the source?* — gets to the heart of what frequency actually means, in a way that the formula Δf=fv/cΔf=fv/c absolutely does not.

Worth noting: if the wires are long enough (a metre and a half is good) the shift is about 11–12 Hz at 300 Hz — close to a semitone. Good musicians in the room will notice.

What resonance feels like

Before Tacoma Narrows (and yes, you should show the Tacoma Narrows clip — it remains one of the most dramatic pieces of footage in all of physics teaching), try to give students a felt sense of resonance first.

The hacksaw blade in a clamp is one of the best demonstrations in this section of the A-level curriculum. Mount it vertically, load the top with plasticine, and press a vibration generator against the lower end. Scan up through frequencies. When resonance hits, the amplitude is startling — the blade suddenly and violently commits to its motion in a way that looks almost alive.

Change the plasticine load. The resonant frequency shifts. Why? Because you’ve changed the system’s natural frequency. The driving frequency now needs to find it again. This is the entire principle of resonance, lived through a hacksaw blade and a lump of grey putty.

For a gentler version that works from Year 9 upwards, Barton’s pendulums are unbeatable. The heavy pendulum drives the system; only the light pendulum of matching length responds fully. The others move — but something about the matching, the sympathy of the identical length, makes one of them swing with a kind of authority the others lack.

The word resonance comes from the Latin resonare, to resound. Worth telling them that.

Sound is not invisible

One of the persistent difficulties in teaching sound is that we ask students to believe in something they can’t see. Here is a short list of things you can do to make sound visible, tangible, or otherwise physical.

A vibrating tuning fork, lightly touched to the surface of water in a tray, produces ripples. Not metaphorical ripples — actual, spreading, circular ripples. The fork is a disturbance. The water surface shows you what that disturbance does.

A small polystyrene ball on a thread, barely touching a struck tuning fork, is flung away with surprising violence. The fork is vibrating. This is the evidence. And if you ask students to calculate the energy stored in that motion from the mass of the ball and its swing — you get something absurdly small. A tuning fork is doing extraordinary mechanical work at frequencies we can barely detect.

Perhaps the most dramatic demonstration of sound as a physical phenomenon is the Rijke tube — a vertical metal pipe with a piece of copper gauze near the bottom, heated until red-hot and then left to cool. As it cools, the contracting air drives the column into resonance and the tube sings, loudly, for a sustained note that lasts long after the flame is removed. There is something almost supernatural about it the first time. The cooling metal is doing work. The air is oscillating. The tube is choosing its fundamental frequency from its own geometry.

This is physics as magic trick — except the trick is just thermodynamics.

The one experiment that always works

If I could only keep one demonstration from this entire section of the curriculum, it would be the wine glass.

Moisten a finger with a tiny amount of methylated spirit to remove the grease. Run it steadily around the rim of a half-full wine glass. Apply consistent, moderate pressure. After a moment’s patience, the glass begins to sing.

You can feel the vibration. The water inside starts to move — first faintly, then into proper standing wave patterns that students at the back of the room can see. Change the water level and the pitch shifts. A row of glasses with different amounts of liquid becomes an instrument.

It works every time. It costs nothing. And it takes the abstract — frequency, resonance, amplitude, standing waves — and makes it something you can produce with your own hands, on an object everyone has encountered, by doing something that feels almost like playing music.

Which, of course, it is.

A note on the history

The wave theory of light was not obvious. Huygens proposed it in 1678 and it was largely ignored — Newton’s particle theory held the field for over a century. Young’s double-slit experiment in 1801 was the turning point, producing interference fringes that particles simply couldn’t explain. But even then, the result was contested.

Maxwell’s equations in 1865 went further: light was not just wave-like, it was a wave — oscillating electric and magnetic fields, self-propagating, requiring no medium at all. Twenty years later, Hertz confirmed it experimentally.

The history matters because it reminds students — and us — that the wave model is not self-evident. It was hard-won, contested, and required generations of physicists to establish. The equations we write on the board are the distillation of that effort. They deserve some ceremony.
