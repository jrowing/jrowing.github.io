Understanding radioactivity did not begin as a technical problem. Long before cloud chambers and Geiger counters, before half-lives and decay chains, there was a more fundamental unease: the suspicion that matter might not be as solid and static as it appeared. The Roman poet and philosopher Lucretius, writing in the first century BCE, gave voice to that unease in De Rerum Natura — On the Nature of Things1.

Lucretius believed that everything was made of indivisible particles moving through the void. These atoms were eternal, but their arrangements were not. Worlds formed and dissolved. Structures decayed. Nothing was fixed forever.

“Thus nature works, and by no compact keeps
The same forms always.”

What Lucretius lacked was evidence.

By the end of the nineteenth century, physics thought it had finally escaped such uncertainty. Nature, it seemed, had settled down. Atoms were solid. Energy was conserved. Time ran smoothly forward. The universe was intelligible and, above all, stable. Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) went so far in in the late 1800’s as to state;

“There is nothing new to be discovered in physics now. All that remains is more and more precise measurement,”

Then matter began to glow in the dark…

Wilhelm Röntgen (1845–1923) was a German physicist working at the University of Würzburg in 1895. He was experimenting with cathode ray tubes, glass tubes in which electrons were accelerated and struck a metal surface, producing mysterious fluorescent glows. While investigating these effects, Röntgen noticed that a nearby fluorescent screen began to glow, even though it was shielded from the tube and the visible light.

Intrigued, he performed a series of careful experiments2. He discovered that a new kind of invisible radiation was being emitted by the cathode ray tube, capable of penetrating opaque materials like paper, wood, and even human tissue, but leaving denser materials like bones to cast a shadow. Röntgen immediately realised the medical potential of this phenomenon.

On December 22, 1895, Röntgen famously took an X-ray photograph of his wife, Bertha’s, hand. The image clearly showed the bones of her hand and her wedding ring, stunning the scientific community. He called the unknown rays “X-rays”, using the mathematical “X” to denote something unknown.

Röntgen’s discovery caused an immediate sensation, both in science and in the wider public. Within months, X-rays were being used to take all sorts of novelty picture as well as in hospitals to image fractures, foreign objects, and other internal structures. For this groundbreaking work, Röntgen was awarded the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901.

Intrigued by the recent discovery of X-rays, a French physicist working in Paris at the turn of the 20th century called Henri Becquerel (1852–1908) started wondering whether fluorescent or phosphorescent materials might emit a similar kind of invisible radiation3.

It was initially thought that these minerals needed to be exposed the plates to sunlight to stimulate the phosphorescence, so In early 1896, Bhe wrapped photographic plates in black paper to shield them from sunlight, ready to place the “charged” uranium salts on top, reasoning that if the salts emitted X-ray-like rays, they would fog the plates.

But then Paris weather turned cloudy. Becquerel, expecting a pause in the experiment, stored the plates in a drawer with the uranium salts.

The fantastic bit of science was that when he remembered the experiment later he thought to develop the photographic plates just in case. Despite not expecting it, he found strong images, despite no sunlight exposure. The uranium had emitted radiation spontaneously, without any external stimulation.

This was the first observation of natural radioactivity. Becquerel realized that the emission came from the uranium itself — a property of the atom, not of its chemical environment or exposure to light. It was a shocking discovery, challenging the notion that atoms were immutable.

Becquerel quickly published his results in early 1896. His discovery caught the attention of other scientists, including Marie and Pierre Curie, who expanded the study to other elements and coined the term radioactivity. Becquerel shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with the Curies for this foundational work.

With this inspiration Marie Skłodowska Curie (1867–1934), a Polish-born physicist and chemist working in Paris with her husband Pierre Curie, entered the scene. Fascinated by the strange rays that Becquerel had observed, Marie decided to investigate whether other elements might also emit radiation.

She began a painstaking and systematic study of uranium ores, particularly pitchblende, aiming to quantify the radiation emitted by different substances. Using highly sensitive electrometers, she and Pierre measured the intensity of radiation. In doing so, they noticed something remarkable: certain samples of pitchblende were far more radioactive than could be explained by the uranium content alone. This suggested the existence of previously unknown, intensely radioactive elements hidden in the ore.

In 1898, after months of grueling laboratory work, the Curies announced the discovery of polonium, named after Marie’s native Poland, and radium, named for the Latin word for ray. Extracting these elements was a monumental task: tons of pitchblende were processed, acids and precipitations were repeated countless times, and the laboratory was poorly ventilated — the dangers of radiation were not yet understood. Radium, in particular, glowed with a faint blue light, capturing public imagination and symbolizing the mysterious power of radioactivity.

Marie Curie’s work on thorium was also significant. Thorium had been studied previously, but Curie’s meticulous measurements demonstrated that thorium, like uranium, emitted spontaneous radiation, making it the first element other than uranium to be recognized as naturally radioactive.

The discovery of these new elements had profound consequences. Radium became a tool for medical therapy (see This Substack of mine), polonium highlighted the potential for atomic transformation, and the work with thorium helped solidify the idea that radioactivity was a property of the atom itself.

Marie Curie’s extraordinary persistence and precision in isolating these elements, despite the severe risks and hardships, earned her two Nobel Prizes: Physics in 1903 (shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel) and Chemistry in 1911, making her the only person to receive Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields. Her work laid the foundation for modern nuclear science and medicine, and her discoveries remain central to our understanding of atomic physics. Marie Curie would eventually die from radiation-induced illness, her notebooks still radioactive today.

Radioactivity was already teaching its first lesson: nature does not reveal her secrets without cost.

Ernest Rutherford (1871–1937), a New Zealand-born physicist, and Frederick Soddy (1877–1956), an English chemist, set out to investigate the mysterious emissions from radioactive substances. They were particularly troubled by the question of Where did the energy come from? Was the atom truly indivisible, or was there something more inside?. Working at McGill University in Montreal, they performed a series of meticulous experiments measuring the properties of alpha and beta radiation.

Through their studies, Rutherford and Soddy made a startling realization: radioactive elements were changing into other elements over time. Uranium, for example, was slowly transforming into thorium. This was a radical idea — the atom, long thought to be permanent and immutable, was in fact disintegrating. This was transmutation, once the dream of alchemists, now written into equations and decay curves. They formulated the disintegration theory, showing that the radiation and the energy somehow came from the atom itself, not from chemical reactions.

Soddy introduced the concept of the half-life, a measure of the time it takes for half the atoms in a radioactive sample to decay. This provided a way to quantify the decay process mathematically and predictably. Together, Rutherford and Soddy demonstrated that atomic transformation was natural and inevitable — atoms had life cycles, and matter itself was not as permanent as once thought.

Rutherford further classified radiation into three types: alpha particles (helium nuclei), beta particles (electrons), and gamma rays (high-energy photons). This categorization helped scientists understand the nature of radioactive decay and laid the groundwork for the modern nuclear model of the atom.

The work of Rutherford and Soddy fundamentally reshaped physics and chemistry. Not only did it explain the source of energy in radioactive materials, but it also opened the door to the concept of nuclear energy and transmutation, ideas that would later underpin nuclear power and weapons. Their discoveries are considered the birth of nuclear physics.

By 1909, Ernest Rutherford had already transformed the study of radioactivity, but the structure of the atom itself remained mysterious. Scientists still imagined atoms as solid, indivisible spheres with their positive charge spread evenly throughout. Rutherford suspected this model was too simple and devised an experiment to test it.

Working with his assistants Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden at the University of Manchester, Rutherford set up a delicate experiment: a thin sheet of gold foil was bombarded with alpha particles — the positively charged particles emitted by radioactive substances. Surrounding the foil was a fluorescent screen that would flash when struck by alpha particles, allowing the team to track the particles’ paths.

Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, as if the atoms were mostly empty space. But to everyone’s astonishment, a tiny fraction of particles bounced back, some at angles greater than 90 degrees. These deflections were shocking — like firing a cannonball at a piece of tissue paper and having it bounce back at you.

Rutherford realized the implications immediately: the atom was not a uniform, solid sphere. Instead, nearly all its mass and positive charge were concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus at the center. The electrons orbited this nucleus at relatively vast distances, leaving the rest of the atom as empty space.

This experiment, later called the gold foil experiment or Rutherford scattering, fundamentally changed the understanding of atomic structure. It introduced the concept of the nucleus, explained the behavior of radioactive emissions, and laid the foundation for the Bohr model of the atom and modern nuclear physics.

Rutherford famously remarked on the surprise of the results: “It was as incredible as if you had fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.”

The experiment revealed the atom to be mostly empty space with a compact core, forever changing our understanding of matter.

By the late 1930s, scientists had learned that splitting heavy nuclei could release enormous amounts of energy. In 1938, Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann achieved nuclear fission of uranium, and Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch provided the theoretical explanation. The implications were staggering: energy from a single atom could rival tons of conventional explosives.

World War II transformed this scientific knowledge into a matter of strategic urgency. Both the Allies and the Axis powers recognized that nuclear fission could be weaponized. In the United States, the Manhattan Project was launched in 1942, a massive, secret effort to develop atomic bombs using uranium and plutonium — a synthetic radioactive element produced in nuclear reactors. Key facilities were built in Los Alamos, Oak Ridge, and Hanford, combining physicists, chemists, and engineers in an unprecedented industrial-scientific effort.

Meanwhile, Germany had its own nuclear program, attempting to harness uranium and heavy water for energy and potentially weapons, though it never achieved a working bomb. The race was on: radioactive materials were no longer just objects of curiosity in laboratories; they were strategic resources, with the power to reshape warfare and geopolitics.

In 1945, radioactive materials were used for the first time in warfare when the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, unleashing not only unprecedented explosive destruction but also long-lasting radiation that caused immediate deaths and lingering illnesses. These events demonstrated the dual nature of radioactive materials: their potential for both technological progress and catastrophic human harm.

The decades that followed lived in radioactivity’s long shadow. Nuclear power promised abundance. Nuclear weapons promised annihilation. Accidents and fallout made radiation synonymous with invisible death.

And yet radioactivity never left us. It had always been there — in rocks, in air, in our own bodies. Life itself evolved under a rain of background radiation, adapting rather than retreating.

When scientists began measuring radioactive decay with ever-greater sensitivity in the mid-20th century, they encountered a persistent problem: background radiation. Naturally occurring radioactive isotopes in soil, building materials, and even the air could interfere with delicate experiments, making it hard to detect extremely faint signals and, thanks to nuclear testing, almost all our materials are contaminated in the most minuscule of ways. To overcome this, physicists looked for materials that were exceptionally low in radioactivity. One remarkable solution came from an unexpected place: ancient Roman lead. Lead recovered from Roman shipwrecks or archaeological sites had been underwater or buried for nearly two millennia, plenty long enough before nuclear testing to have avoided contamination.

This ancient lead has extremely low background count, providing a clean material for shielding sensitive detectors. In experiments searching for rare nuclear processes — such as double beta decay, neutrino interactions, or dark matter detection — Roman lead allows scientists to dramatically reduce background noise. Its purity made it a prized resource, bridging the gap between ancient craftsmanship and cutting-edge physics. Of course, historians might argue not to damage and destroy priceless history in the name of physics - and quite rightly so, you’ll have to judge for yourself if using the lead or preserving it is preferable.

Today, radioactivity works mostly in silence. It diagnoses disease, dates ancient bones, traces groundwater, powers spacecraft. We measure it carefully, regulate it obsessively, and still argue about what risks we are willing to accept.

In recent years, the study of lanthanides and other rare-earth elements has uncovered new radioactive isotopes with subtle and surprising properties. These discoveries are opening fresh avenues in quantum materials, medical imaging, and even sustainable energy technologies, showing that the story of radioactivity is still being written — not just in history books, but in modern laboratories.

Radioactivity forces us to think in timescales that dwarf politics and lifetimes. Half-lives stretch beyond empires and waste demands responsibility from people not yet born. With those thoughts of the future I’ll leave you with the Ray Cat.

Eugène-Melchior Peligot (1811–1890) was a French chemist who worked in the laboratory of the famous chemist Antoine-François de Fourcroy. In the late 1780s, pitchblende — a dense black mineral containing uranium oxides — had been studied by Martin Heinrich Klaproth, a German chemist, who had first isolated uranium in 1789. However, Klaproth’s sample was not entirely pure, and the methods for isolating the element were still rudimentary.

Peligot improved upon these early methods. Using careful chemical separation techniques, he successfully isolated uranium in its metallic form from pitchblende, confirming its existence as a distinct chemical element. His work clarified the composition of uranium compounds and helped establish uranium as a new element of the periodic table.

While Peligot’s work predated the discovery of radioactivity by more than a century, it was essential because it provided a pure, well-characterized substance that later scientists, such as Henri Becquerel and the Curies, could study. Without Peligot’s isolation of uranium, the subsequent observations of its mysterious energy emissions — the first hints of radioactivity — might have been much harder to interpret.
