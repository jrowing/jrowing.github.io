A Scottish ghost

September 1894, a young Scottish physicist called Charles Thomson Rees Wilson — C.T.R. Wilson to his colleagues — spent a few weeks working as a temporary observer at the meteorological station on the summit of Ben Nevis. He was twenty-five years old. The job was unpaid. He took it for the love of weather.

One morning, walking on the summit in mist, he saw a Brocken spectre.

If you have not encountered one, it is one of the strangest sights nature offers: a vast, ghostly human figure looming in the cloud, surrounded by a rainbow halo. The figure is, in fact, your own shadow, projected onto the mist by the low sun behind you, and the halo is the diffraction of sunlight by water droplets. Goethe put a Brocken spectre into Faust as the form of Lord Urian, who presides over the witches’ sabbath on the mountain of the same name in the Harz. Hikers in Scotland and the Alps still see them today, occasionally, when the weather plays right.

Wilson was captivated. He went back to the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge with the firm intention of recreating those optical effects in the lab — the colours, the halos, the mist. He built a small chamber in which moist air could be made to expand suddenly, cooling and condensing into a tiny cloud. He had no interest in particle physics. He had not, at this point, even encountered the idea of an ionising particle, because none had yet been discovered.

This was 1894. X-rays would not be discovered until late 1895. Radioactivity would not be discovered until 1896. The electron would not be identified until 1897. Wilson was, at the moment he began the work, building an instrument that would eventually reveal a world of phenomena that nobody yet knew existed.

A useful accident

Within a few months of building his expansion chamber, Wilson noticed something odd. Even with all the dust filtered out of the air, a few stubborn droplets kept forming each time he expanded the chamber. He suspected — correctly — that these droplets were condensing on ions, on charged molecules left behind by whatever was producing the residual electrical conductivity of the atmosphere.

When the X-ray was discovered, Wilson immediately tried his apparatus on it. The X-rays poured into the chamber, the chamber filled with mist, and Wilson realised he had built an instrument that could make ionising radiation visible. He spent the next fifteen years refining it.

By 1911 he had a chamber sensitive enough to photograph individual particle tracks — the first human being to see, directly, the path of a single alpha particle, a single electron, a single anything. Rutherford, who was not given to hyperbole, called it “the most original and wonderful instrument in scientific history”. Wilson was awarded the Nobel Prize for it in 1927.

What I love about that story is its shape. The most important particle physics instrument of the early twentieth century began with a young man on a Scottish mountain, captivated by a ghost. He did not set out to do particle physics. He set out to recreate a Brocken spectre. The particle physics was a side effect of trying to understand the weather.

The chamber that saw new particles

The cloud chamber went on to a remarkable career. It is the instrument that first revealed almost every new kind of subatomic particle in the decades that followed.

In 1932, a young American called Carl Anderson at Caltech was photographing cosmic rays in a cloud chamber when he noticed a track that looked, in every respect, like an electron — except that it curved the wrong way in the magnetic field. An electron with a positive charge. He had photographed the first antimatter particle ever observed, the positron, whose existence had been predicted by Paul Dirac a few years earlier from a strange asymmetry in the equations of quantum mechanics. Anderson got the Nobel Prize for it in 1936.

A few years later, Patrick Blackett and Giuseppe Occhialini, working at Cambridge, photographed pair production and pair annihilation in their cloud chamber: a gamma ray turning, in front of the camera, into an electron and a positron, which then went their separate ways and eventually met other particles and turned back into gamma rays. Matter being created from energy and dissolving back into it, on a photographic plate. E = mc² in a single image;

Then came the muon, the kaon, the strange particles, the lambda. Every one of them first photographed in a cloud chamber. The chamber was, for thirty years, the primary instrument by which humanity discovered what the universe is made of.

It is no longer the state of the art. Bubble chambers and silicon detectors and the immense apparatus of the LHC have long since overtaken it. But every modern particle detector is, at heart, the same idea Wilson hit on at Ben Nevis: a medium in which a passing particle leaves a visible disturbance. Different medium, different disturbance, same principle.

In your kitchen

If you have not built one of these, you should. The recipe is undignified in its simplicity.

You need a transparent container, a piece of black felt, some isopropyl alcohol, a metal plate, a slab of dry ice, and a source. The source can be a school-stock alpha from the prep room, or — with appropriate care and within the legal limits for educational use — the americium button from a domestic smoke detector. Stick the felt to the lid, soak it in alcohol, invert the container over the plate, and rest the whole thing on the dry ice. Wait fifteen minutes. Shine a torch in from the side.

These instructions are great: https://scoollab.web.cern.ch/cloud-chamber

The first tracks usually appear within seconds of the chamber stabilising. Long, fast, straight lines from cosmic-ray muons. Fans of alpha tracks from your source, like the ones in the photograph at the top of this post. The occasional kinked beta. If you have a piece of thoriated welding rod or some uranium glaze nearby, more of them.

The total cost is around thirty pounds. It runs for an hour on a single block of dry ice. And it puts in front of your students the same view of the universe that Wilson saw at the Cavendish in 1911, that Anderson saw at Caltech in 1932, that Blackett saw at Cambridge in 1933. The same instrument, in essentials. The same kind of moment.

A nucleus, choosing to fall apart

Each line in my photograph is a single alpha particle. Each alpha particle came from a single nucleus, somewhere in the source, choosing a particular moment in the past few seconds to fall apart. There is no signal that preceded it. There is nothing about the nucleus that decayed that distinguishes it from the millions around it that did not.

We tell students that radioactive decay is random. We mean it in the strongest possible sense: not the random of a die roll, which only looks random because we cannot see the initial conditions, but the deep random of quantum mechanics, with no hidden parameter that would let you predict the next event. Saying it is one thing. Showing it is another.

The chamber turns radioactive decay from an equation into an event. It does the same for cosmic rays, for pair production, for the entire shadow world of particles that surrounds us all the time and which we almost never see. It is, as Rutherford said, the most original and wonderful instrument in scientific history. And it began with a young Scottish physicist watching his own shadow on a mountain in the mist.

I cannot think of a story I would rather tell my students.
