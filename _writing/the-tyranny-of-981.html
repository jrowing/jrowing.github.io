There is a number every physics teacher writes on the board hundreds of times a year. It appears in GCSE energy calculations, in projectile problems, in the definition of weight, in the design of every roller-coaster question we set. It looks, written down, like a fact of nature.

It is not.

g = 9.81 m/s² is one of the most quietly misleading numbers in school science. Not because it is wrong — but because the way we present it conceals where it comes from, what it actually is, and why it varies.

It is a useful number. It is a defensible number. But it is not, in any actual sense, a constant.

The first thing worth saying out loud is that g varies with latitude. At the equator, g is about 9.78 m/s². At the poles, it is about 9.83 m/s². That is a difference of around half a percent — small enough to ignore in most school calculations, large enough to matter to anyone weighing gold, designing a ballistic missile, or competing at the Olympics. The 1968 long jump world record set in Mexico City has a small but real assist from a lower local g (and a thinner atmosphere, but let us not get distracted).

Two effects combine to produce this latitude dependence. First, the Earth is not a sphere; it is an oblate spheroid, slightly squashed, so a person at the equator stands further from the centre of mass than a person at the pole. Second, the Earth rotates, and the centripetal acceleration required to keep an object moving in a circle at the equator must be subtracted from the gravitational pull to give the apparent g a set of bathroom scales would measure. Both effects pull in the same direction: equatorial g is smaller, polar g is larger.

g also varies with altitude, in the obvious way. Climb Snowdon and you weigh fractionally less; fly to the Himalayas and your aeroplane meals are fractionally lighter. It varies with what is underneath you: stand over a salt dome and g drops a little; stand on dense basalt and it rises. Geophysicists exploit this; their gravimeters are sensitive enough to map oilfields and buried cavities by reading the local distortions in g. There are even tiny tidal contributions from the Moon and Sun overhead, oscillating g at the seventh decimal place.

So the question worth putting to a class is not just what is g? but whose g are we talking about?

Here is where the story takes a slightly awkward turn, because the answer is: somebody else’s.

The figure we drill into students — 9.81, or more formally 9.80665 m/s² — was fixed by international agreement at the third General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1901. It was based on measurements made in 1888 at the Pavillon de Breteuil, the small chateau in Sèvres, just outside Paris, that houses the International Bureau of Weights and Measures. The Paris value was then adjusted, theoretically, to what g would be at latitude 45° at sea level — a notional point lying somewhere in the Bay of Biscay, the Po Valley, or the cornfields of Minnesota, depending which longitude you walk along.

In other words: the gravity we teach in Bolton is the gravity of a French aristocrat’s garden.

It is worth sitting with this for a moment. The metric system itself is a child of the French Revolution, defined with a confident universalism that has not entirely aged well. The metre was originally one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the North Pole through Paris — through Paris, naturally, not through Greenwich or Beijing. The kilogram, until 2019, was a lump of platinum-iridium alloy in a vault in Sèvres. And our standard g, the one every British student calculates with, is the gravity at that same vault, lightly massaged.

There is something quietly post-imperial about all of this. The SI system presents itself as universal, neutral, placeless — the language of science as such. But its zero point, its reference, its physical embodiment, all sit within a few miles of one another in the suburbs of Paris. We export this geography back to ourselves every time we write g = 9.81.

The real value of g in London is closer to 9.8119 m/s². Captain Henry Kater measured something very close to this with a pendulum in 1818, more than half a century before Defforges took the measurement near Paris that would eventually become the world’s standard. London’s gravity is, fractionally, heavier than the value we use; the SI g leaves us slightly buoyant in our own classrooms. Edinburgh is heavier still. Penzance fractionally lighter. None of this matters for a GCSE calculation, but all of it matters for the philosophical question of what we are doing when we round to 9.81 and call it true.

This matters pedagogically, I think, for three reasons.

The first is honesty. Students often arrive at A-level physics with a vague sense that the textbook is a list of facts, and that learning physics is the act of memorising them. The slow disabusement of this notion is one of the more important things sixth form does. Telling them that g is not a fact but an agreement — useful, principled, but contingent — is a small step in that direction. The same conversation can be had about boiling points (whose pressure?), Ohm’s law (which materials, at which temperatures?), and the periodic table (whose conventions for the lanthanides?). Constants are conventions wearing better tailoring.

The second is precision. At A-level, we expect students to discuss uncertainties. We give them error bars, lines of best and worst fit, percentage uncertainties on their own measurements. Then we hand them a value of g with five significant figures and no quoted uncertainty, as if it had descended fully formed from Olympus. It seems to me a small but worthwhile exercise to ask a class: if you could measure g in this lab, right now, would you expect to get 9.81? The answer is no, and the reasons why — your local geology, your altitude, your latitude, the imperfections of your pendulum — are exactly the reasons we want them to think about in their own practical work.

The third is curiosity. The question why is g different in different places? is an honest, generative question that opens onto the shape of the Earth, the rotation of the Earth, the density of the Earth, the tides, the metric system, the politics of measurement, and the difference between gravitational and apparent gravity in a non-inertial frame. It is, in the proper sense, a physics question. It is also a question that the standard treatment of g — write it on the board, plug it in, move on — does its level best to suppress.

I am not arguing that we should teach 9.78 in Singapore, 9.81 in London, and 9.83 in Tromsø, though it is amusing to imagine the inset chart on a worldwide GCSE specification. The pragmatic value of a single agreed number is real. What I am arguing is that we should treat the agreement as an agreement — say so, briefly, when we introduce it — and that we should occasionally invite students to wonder what is being smoothed over.

A simple opener: bring a high-precision smartphone accelerometer (Phyphox will do it) into the lab, lie it flat, and let it sit. Read off what it claims to be measuring. It will not be 9.81. It will be whatever your building, your latitude, and your local rocks conspire to produce.

And as always - ask students why...
