Reflection in teaching is often talked about as if its value were self-evident. Teachers are encouraged to “be reflective”, to write reflections for appraisal, or to discuss lessons afterwards — sometimes without much clarity about why this should help, or under what conditions it actually does.

The evidence suggests that reflection can be powerful CPD, but only when it does a specific job.

At its core, reflection works because it turns experience into learning. Simply having classroom experience — even lots of it — does not reliably improve teaching. What matters is whether teachers systematically think about what happened, why it happened, and what they might do differently next time. Research across teacher education and professional learning consistently finds that it is this analytical step, rather than the experience itself, that drives improvement.

Importantly, not all reflection is equally effective. Descriptive reflection — simply recounting what happened in a lesson — has little impact on practice. What seems to matter is structured reflection that focuses attention on decisions, evidence, and consequences. When teachers are prompted to ask questions such as “What did students actually do?”, “What evidence do I have that they learned?”, and “What will I change next time?”, reflection becomes a mechanism for refining practice rather than a narrative exercise.

There is also evidence that reflection supports deeper professional learning over time. Studies of teacher development show that reflective teachers gradually build richer mental models of teaching — they become better at noticing what matters in classrooms, anticipating difficulties, and selecting appropriate responses. In other words, reflection helps teachers move from reacting to events towards making more principled, informed decisions.

Reflection appears to be particularly effective when it is connected to real classroom practice rather than abstract ideas. CPD that asks teachers to reflect on their own students, their own lessons, and their own evidence of learning is more likely to influence what happens next in the classroom. This helps explain why brief, regular reflection embedded into normal teaching routines often outperforms one-off training sessions with no follow-up.

Collaborative reflection strengthens this further. When teachers reflect with colleagues — through discussion, coaching, or shared analysis of lessons — they are more likely to challenge assumptions, consider alternative interpretations, and translate insights into concrete changes. The social element matters: reflection becomes less about self-justification and more about problem-solving.

That said, reflection is not a magic ingredient. On its own, it does not replace subject knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, or well-designed instruction. Reflection works best when teachers have access to ideas, evidence, or models to reflect with. Without that input, reflection risks reinforcing existing habits rather than improving them.

The strongest conclusion from the evidence is therefore a modest but important one: reflection is effective CPD when it is purposeful, structured, and tied to action. It is the process that connects experience to improvement. Used well, it helps teachers learn not just from teaching, but about teaching — and then do something different as a result.

At the end of last year I moved from teaching in a highly specialised environment to teaching all the sciences in a small school, in a different country. It’s been a change in almost every sense: curriculum, culture, resources, pace. The sort of change that forces you to notice what you’re relying on, because many of the usual supports simply aren’t there.

What I’ve found myself doing, repeatedly, is checking which of my teaching instincts survive contact with a new context. Which ideas were artefacts of a particular school, or a particular set of students, and which ones seem to hold even when everything else shifts.

Some things turned out to be remarkably stable.

The first is that clarity matters more than almost anything else. Students cope far better when they know exactly what they’re trying to do and what success looks like. That sounds obvious, but it’s easy to drift into vagueness when you’re comfortable, or to overcompensate with enthusiasm when you’re new.

I also re-learned, fairly quickly, that talking more does not help students learn more. When you’re teaching new material, or teaching outside your key specialism, there’s a strong temptation to explain everything. Each time I resisted that urge — breaking explanations into smaller pieces and checking understanding more often — learning improved. The limits of working memory are impressively indifferent to good intentions.

Retrieval practice, too, turned out to be stubbornly reliable. Short, low-stakes recall at the start of lessons worked just as well here as it ever does. No elaborate systems, no fancy resources. Just questions that forced students to think about what they’d previously encountered. When something works across countries, subjects, and age groups, it’s usually because it’s aligned with how memory actually functions.

Across physics, chemistry, and biology, the most powerful moments tended to come when I modelled thinking explicitly. Not just how to get an answer, but how to choose an approach, what to check, where mistakes are likely. Students don’t struggle because they haven’t seen enough correct solutions; they struggle because they lack the experience of being wrong. By the time we get to be a teacher we have made-and learnt from - every mistake going, we can best transfer that experience by making sure our thinking is laid bare.

Resource constraints had an oddly positive effect -

Practical lessons create opportunities that no explanation can fully substitute for: confronting misconceptions, anchoring abstract ideas in something tangible, and giving students a reason to care about precision.

But practical work only earns its keep when it’s treated as more than activity. The most effective practicals this term were pared down, tightly structured, with a clear purpose, careful modelling, and deliberate follow-up. Students learned most when experiments were used to generate questions, test predictions, or explain results — not when they were left to “discover” ideas unaided.

Teaching practical science in a resource-limited context sharpened this further. When equipment is scarce, you’re forced to be intentional. Every practical has to justify itself. That constraint turned out to be a strength: fewer activities, better chosen, more carefully framed.

Feedback followed a similar pattern. The most effective feedback I gave was not the most detailed, but the most focused. A small number of specific prompts that required students to act, rather than a page of corrections that allowed them to remain passive. If feedback doesn’t cause thinking, it’s just noise.

. Simpler slides, cleaner worksheets, fewer decorative elements. Not because of any grand principle, but because there wasn’t time or infrastructure for anything else. The result was clearer instruction and fewer distractions. Cognitive load theory continues to earn its keep.

And finally, I was reminded that learning still looks messy. The lessons that felt smooth and polished were not always the ones where students learned the most. The real progress often came from moments of uncertainty, error, and effort — in practical work as much as in written tasks. Productive struggle remains deeply unfashionable, but stubbornly necessary.

What changed this term was almost everything on the surface. What didn’t change was how people learn. Teaching all the sciences in a very different context has, if anything, made the underlying principles clearer rather than less so.

At the end of a first term that felt unfamiliar in all the right ways, that’s been a reassuring thing to rediscover.
