Basic Astronomy Theory

Key Definitions

  • Astronomy is the scientific study of celestial objects, space, and the physical universe as a whole.

  • Astrophysics applies the laws of physics to explain astronomical observations.

Celestial Objects

  • Stars: Massive, luminous balls of plasma undergoing nuclear fusion.

  • Planets: Objects orbiting a star, spherical due to gravity, and have cleared their orbit.

  • Moons: Natural satellites orbiting planets.

  • Asteroids and Comets: Small Solar System bodies; comets contain ice and dust.

  • Galaxies: Massive systems of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter bound by gravity.

The Solar System

  • The Sun is a main sequence star.

  • Inner planets (Mercury to Mars) are rocky.

  • Outer planets (Jupiter to Neptune) are gas/ice giants.

  • Orbits are elliptical: governed by Kepler’s Laws.

Kepler’s Laws of Planetary Motion

  1. First Law (Elliptical Orbits): Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.

  2. Second Law (Equal Areas): A line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

  3. Third Law (Harmonic Law):r3T2=constantwhere r is the semi-major axis and T is the orbital period.

Newton’s Law of Gravitation

The gravitational force between two masses is:F=Gm1m2r2

  • Where G is the gravitational constant: 6.674×10−11Nm2/kg2

  • F is always attractive and acts along the line joining the masses.

Orbits and Circular Motion

For a body in circular orbit around a mass M:

  • Orbital speed: v=GMr

  • Orbital period: T=2πrv=2πr3GM

Light and Observation

  • Spectroscopy is used to determine a star’s composition and motion.

  • Redshift occurs when objects move away, shifting spectral lines to longer wavelengths:z=λobs−λrestλrest

  • The Doppler effect allows us to measure radial velocity:Δλλ=vc

The Expanding Universe

  • Hubble’s Law: Galaxies recede with velocity proportional to distance:v=H0dwhere H0 is the Hubble constant.

  • Evidence for the Big Bang includes:

    • Redshift of galaxies

    • Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB)

    • Abundance of light elements (H, He)

Life Cycle of Stars

  • Low mass stars: Nebula → Protostar → Main sequence → Red giant → White dwarf

  • High mass stars: Nebula → Protostar → Main sequence → Supergiant → Supernova → Neutron star / Black hole

Stories to enrich

I suppose you think there was a The “Big Bang”?

The name “Big Bang” was given to the theory by British astronomer Fred Hoyle in 1949. He used the term during a BBC radio broadcast to describe the theory that the universe originated from a state of high density and temperature and expanded rapidly. Hoyle, a proponent of the steady-state theory, used the term to mock the expansionist theory of Georges Lemaître. Despite its origin, the term stuck and became the widely accepted name for the theory that the universe began in a state of extreme density and temperature.

“White Dielectric Material”

Robert Penzias and Arno Wilson were interested in measuring the intensity of radiation from a supernova remnant and went aboutsetting up a horn antenna with a device to filter out noise by comparing the collected signal against a cold load, a Dewar of liquid helium at 4.2K. They were surprised to detect a microwave signal across all areas of the sky. In looking for sources of the noise they evicted some pigeons from the telescope and cleaned off what Penzias described as a ‘white dielectric material’ left by the birds…

The cleaning, however, failed to eliminate the noise from the signal and in 1978 they were awarded the Nobel prize for their part in the discovery of the cosmic microwave background radiation.

In space, nobody can hear you snore…

That’s not at all true, unless you slept outside but Astronauts on the International Space Station do have to sleep by a ventilator. The microgravity on the spacecraft means exhaled carbon dioxide does not move away from a sleeping astronaut and, without air circulation, they could suffocate in a bubble of their own carbon dioxide.

Green stars…

For a star to emit most radiation in the green wavelength it would require a surface temperature of around 6000 K. But it would then also emit similar amounts of blue and red light resulting in a white or pale-yellow colour, like the Sun. So there cannot be any green stars.

Listen to the stars

Dr Wanda Diaz Merced, has developed an approach to turning astronomical data into auditory signals, known as sonificiation. It’s well worth listening to her TED talk: https://www.ted.com/talks/wanda_diaz_merced_how_a_blind_astronomer_found_a_way_to_hear_the_stars

Vera Rubin and the Case of the Missing Mass

When Vera Rubin measured the speeds of stars orbiting galaxies, she expected stars farther out to move more slowly (like planets in the Solar System). But they didn’t—they moved at the same speed, defying Newton’s laws.

This wasn’t a mistake. Rubin had uncovered the first convincing evidence for dark matter—a mysterious, invisible substance that makes up most of the universe. Rubin didn’t get a Nobel Prize for this work, though many think she should have, sparking important conversations about recognition of women in science.

When the Universe was dark! 300 000 to one billion years after the Big Bang

As the universe cooled down after the Big Bang a point was reached where the temperature was such that the emitted radiation was in the infra-red - in other words there was no visible light. The Universe became dark.

This was to remain the state until the first stars fused their first hydrogen and this was to happen about one billion years later!

The Star That Blinked – Tabby’s Star

Astronomers using the Kepler Space Telescope found a star, KIC 8462852 (aka Tabby’s Star), with bizarre dips in brightness—sometimes dropping up to 20% or more, which is far too much to be caused by planets.

Wild theories emerged, including the infamous “alien megastructure” hypothesis. The truth is still unclear—probably dust clouds or fragments from broken-up objects—but the mystery drew public imagination.

The Voyager Golden Record

NASA’s Voyager spacecraft carried a golden phonograph record, containing sounds and images from Earth—including greetings in 55 languages, music from Bach to Chuck Berry, and sounds of a baby crying and whales singing.

It was meant as a message to any intelligent life that might find it.

Practical work and Demos

Toilet Paper Solar System

Purpose: Understand the vast distances between planets. How: Use a toilet roll (or a long strip of paper) to mark scaled distances between planets. Note: On this scale most of the planets are too small to see (and draw) – so we’ve cheated and made them 100 times larger than they should be. Earth and Moon are only a few cm apart on the same scale that puts Neptune several meters away.

If the sun is a 60 cm beach ball then…

Volcanoes and heat energy

Although most craters on the Moon and the planets are thought to have been created by meteor impact, some will have been formed by volcanic activity. You can use a saucepan full of custard or porridge being heated to demonstrate the “breakthrough of internal magma”! The air escaping from beneath the surface when they are heated gives a very good simulation of volcanic activity.

Rotation period of the Moon

This simple demonstration shows why it is that the Moon always presents the same face towards the Earth. Two pupils are needed to demonstrate this. One pupil is used to represent the Earth, the other to represent the Moon. One pupil stands still while the second pupil faces inwards and then moves round the first, making one rotation as they orbit the “Earth” once. You will notice that the “Moon” always faces the “Earth”. The cause of this is the eccentricity in the shape of the moon.

Build a comet

Comets are cosmic snowballs of frozen gases, rock and dust, roughly the size of a small town. When a comet’s orbit brings it close to the sun, it heats up and spews dust and gases into a giant glowing head larger than most planets. The dust and gases form a tail that stretches away from the sun for millions of kilometers.

Line the bowl with a plastic bag.

Add to the lined bowl: water, dirt, corn starch, syrup, vinegar and alcohol.

As you do this, explain that comets have lots of ice and water. In our comet model, dirt represents the dust, minerals and water found in comets; corn starch helps hold the model together; the syrup are organic materials and give the comet a dark appearance; vinegar represents amino acids in a comet and the alcohol represents methanol found in comets.

Do not add too much alcohol as it has an antifreeze effect.

Mix ingredients and stir in the dry ice. Once all of the dry ice is in the bowl, pick up the sides of the bag and use them to form the mixture into a large clump. Add more water as needed. The mixture will start to thicken as the dry ice freezes the water. You can feel the clump forming through the plastic bag. If the mixture does not hold together, add more water. Once you see you have a clump, take it out of the bag and show it to students.

At this time, measure the mass of the comet (this can be done using a basic kitchen scale, or on a simple balance if you do not have a triple-beam balance or some other measuring device). Record your data.

You should see gas jets coming off the comet. Repeat the mass measurement in five-minute increments. If your model falls apart, it’s OK. Comets frequently disintegrate as they come closer to the sun! For added effect, have students take turns holding the torch and hairdryer close together and pointing at the comet. The flashlight represents the sun and the dryer is the solar wind. You should see the jets moving away as the heat from the dryer pushes the gas away.

Crater experiment

This experiment is a simulation of meteor impact forming craters on a planet. The meteors are replaced by ball bearings or wooden balls, and a tray of sand replaces the planet surface.

The projectiles are dropped from a known height into the sand and the loss of potential energy is related to the crater size. We measured the diameter, depth, wall height and general shape of the craters and also tried oblique impacts by rolling the ball bearings down a tube. Dampening the sand gives a way of investigating different types of planetary surface.

Model lunar craters

To highlight the way that we can infer information about the moon - and to dispel misconceptions like the “face on mars” we can make our own craters and subsequent shadows. Have students make a clay model of a lunar crater and use a torch to simulate the Sun. A torch can be mounted on a retort stand, and then raised and lowered to see the effects of the altitude of the “Sun” on the lengths of shadows on the “lunar” surface.

Expansion of the Universe

Here are three possible analogies of the expansion of the universe.

  1. A balloon with dots marked on it to represent the galaxies. As the balloon is blown up all the “galaxies” recede from each other.

  2. Draw a wave on a latex exercise band. As the “universe” is stretched, so does the wavelength of the background radiation…

  3. Thread three or four large polystyrene balls on to a length of elastic, one end of which is fixed to a hook on the wall or a secure retort stand. Now pull the free end of the elastic - all the balls separate from each other.

The point about all these analogies is that they make their own space as they expand. Similarly, before the universe began to expand there was no space! Actually there was no time either both space and time were “created” at the start of the universe.

Some simple Observational Astronomy

  • Diameter of the projected image of the Sun to show the varying distance of the Earth from the Sun during a year

  • A sundial

  • Star trails using a camera and an exposure of an hour or more

  • Star pictures using a camera with an exposure of five minutes

  • Sunspots using a projected image of the Sun

  • Length of a shadow at noon at two points on the Earth’s surface lying on a north-south line can be used to measure the size of the Earth (after Eratosthenes).

Danger!

Never look directly at the sun especially through a telescope, binoculars or other lens.

Solar filters are available for events like eclipses however I do not recommend their use in schools. It is better to view a projected image of the Sun when studying sunspots or a solar eclipse. If you can use a smart telescope like the seestar S50 or S30 that’s even better!

Seasons tubes

Use solid rods of yellow foam rubber to represent the sunlight. Cut off the ends at angles that are equal to that of the Sun at the zenith in mid-winter and mid-summer.

In Taunton in mid-winter the maximum angle that the Sun reaches above the horizon is 16 0 while in mid-summer it is 61 0 . The area over which the same ‘tube’ of sunlight is spread is clearly shown by the angled ends of the foam rubber cylinders.

The seasons occur because of the different area of the Earth’s surface over which the sunlight spreads at different times of the year. Use a projector to represent the Sun, a board that can be rotated representing the surface of the Earth to show this change of area, and an LDR to measure the change in light intensity. As the area on which a certain amount of sunlight falls is larger in winter, it is therefore colder.

The minimum age of the Universe

A minimum age for the Universe can be suggested by considering the elements that exist today. In ordinary stars fusion occurs, building up elements as heavy as iron 56 but no further they are not hot enough. Heavier elements are formed in the enormous explosions of supernovae, where the temperatures reached are much greater than those in a normal star. Therefore the Universe must be at least old enough for these supernovae to have formed and exploded, in order to give us the abundance of heavy elements necessary for the Universe to be as we know it today!

Sundial

  1. Prepare the Base Cut a circle from the cardboard or use a full A4 sheet. This will be your dial.

  2. Mark the Centre Use a ruler to find and mark the center of the dial. This is where your gnomon (straw/pencil) will stand.

  3. Set the Gnomon Angle Your gnomon must tilt at an angle equal to your local latitude (e.g., 51° if you’re in London).

Use a protractor to measure this angle from the base. Tape or glue the straw so it points north and leans back at the correct angle from vertical. Make sure the gnomon points parallel to Earth’s axis—this is crucial! 4. Face the Sundial North Place your sundial outside on a flat surface with the gnomon pointing true north. Use a compass to align it. For accuracy: Correct for magnetic declination if needed (in the UK it’s small, <5°). 5. Mark the Hour Lines Each hour, check where the shadow falls. Mark a small line on the dial and label it (e.g. “10am”, “11am”…).

Best to start just before solar noon and go into the afternoon. You can also calculate hour angles and draw them using geometry (see extension below).
